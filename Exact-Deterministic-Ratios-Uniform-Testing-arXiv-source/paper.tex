\begin{abstract}
We study single-machine scheduling with unit-time tests and total completion
time.  A test reveals a job's unknown processing time; without a test, a job
may either be unavailable, run for a common upper bound $u$, or run blindly
for its actual duration, depending on the model.
[tohle je takove suche a nic to podle me nerekne kdyz to clovek nezna. Radsi bych na zacatek dal neco cool]


For deterministic algorithms we determine the exact size-asymptotic ratio
for every common upper bound $u\in(0,\infty]$.  The answer is a continuous
six-piece curve whose cap-free obligatory endpoint is
$1.5705740966\ldots$ and whose global maximum is $1.8667603992\ldots$.
Against an oblivious adversary, we prove the exact randomized finite-cap
curve: it has four pieces, global maximum $1.6257523846\ldots$, and equals
$4/3$ for every $u\ge25/4$.  The curve is the worst-case envelope of a
stronger instance-optimal value for every processing-time multiset.  A
separate uniform argument proves the same sharp $4/3$ value for obligatory
testing with unbounded, unannounced processing times.

For blind execution we prove a stronger instance-optimal statement at the
quadratic scale.  A universal policy learns, from a sublinear pilot, the same
explicit fluid value attainable with advance knowledge of the multiset; a
matching oblivious lower bound holds even for announced adaptive policies.
The proofs combine pairwise delay accounting, completion-density thresholds,
fractional-knapsack envelopes, random relabelling, and sampling without
replacement.

\section{Introduction and proof intuition}
Suppose that several jobs are waiting for one machine.  A job can be
\emph{tested} for one unit of time, after which its processing time becomes
known.  Testing sounds helpful, but its cost is paid at the worst possible
place: while the machine tests one job, every unfinished job waits.  Our
question is how much information or speed-up is worth buying before the
machine should simply start completing jobs.

There are two natural interpretations.  In the \emph{information} view, an
untested job may be run blindly for its true duration; testing only tells us
that duration before we commit to the job.  In the \emph{optimization} view,
an unoptimized job takes a common time $u$, whereas a test unlocks an
optimized duration $p_i\le u$.  Requiring every job to be tested is the clean
common core: it isolates the conflict between learning the instance and
finishing it.  Throughout the paper there is one nonpreemptive machine, all
jobs are available at time zero, and the objective is total completion time.

The three models differ only in what can be done before a test:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
model & untested action & role of a test \\
\midrule
obligatory & none & make the job available \\
blind execution & run for the true $p_i$ & learn $p_i$ before running \\
common upper $u$ & run for time $u$ & unlock processing at the revealed $p_i$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
Our results give exact worst-case ratios in the obligatory and common-upper
models.  They also give a stronger, instance-dependent answer for randomized
algorithms: after a sublinear pilot sample, advance knowledge of the entire
multiset is worth only $o(n^2)$.

\subsection{The common core: obligatory testing} [warmup mi prijde lepsi]

We begin with the most constrained model.  Every job must first undergo its
unit test.  A positive outcome $p_i$ then requires another operation of
length $p_i$; a zero job completes with its test.  After seeing an outcome,
the scheduler must decide whether to process it now or let it wait while more
jobs are tested.

Competitive analysis compares the online cost with the clairvoyant optimum,
which knows all processing times in advance.  We study the leading quadratic
coefficient: an additive $o(n^2)$ loss is ignored.  For deterministic
algorithms this is the usual adversarial game.  For randomized algorithms the
input is fixed before the private random bits are drawn---an \emph{oblivious}
adversary.

\begin{theorem}[Obligatory testing, informal]
The exact size-asymptotic deterministic competitive ratio is
$1.5705740966\ldots$.  Against an oblivious adversary, the exact randomized
ratio is $4/3$, even when processing times are unbounded and their multiset is
not announced.
\end{theorem}

The two optimal algorithms look rather different.  The deterministic
algorithm tests jobs in a fixed order and uses a threshold that changes with
the observed history.  Short outcomes are completed immediately; long ones
are deferred and sorted in a final SPT tail.  The threshold remembers how
many positive jobs have appeared and how much work has already been promised
to the tail.  Its analysis uses a bank account that pays for every pair of
jobs delayed in the wrong order.

The randomized algorithm first samples a sublinear number of jobs.  From the
sample it learns the processing-time prefix with the best
\emph{completion density}: completed mass divided by testing and immediate
processing work.  It then tests the remaining jobs in random order, completes
outcomes below the learned threshold immediately, and defers the rest.  A
fixed cutoff prevents a missed enormous job from contaminating the discovery
phase; if the learned density is too small, the algorithm safely falls back
to testing everything and sorting afterwards.

The lower bounds explain why these choices are necessary.  Against a
deterministic algorithm, decreasing positive levels are revealed from long
to short and a large block of zeros is hidden until the end.  Harmonic gaps
balance the cost of processing a positive job too early against the cost of
deferring it.  Against a randomized algorithm, the hard multiset is much
simpler: half the jobs have length zero and half have length two, with a
uniformly random labelling.  Until many labels have been inspected, every
unit of work completes only about half a job; integrating this completion
envelope gives the factor $4/3$.  The precise statements and proofs appear in
\cref{thm:main-intro,thm:random-obligatory-intro,sec:obligatory,sec:random-obligatory}.

\subsection{Beyond a worst-case ratio: instance optimality}

A single competitive ratio deliberately forgets the instance.  This can be
misleading here.  On some multisets testing everything and sorting is nearly
optimal; on others testing is almost useless.  An algorithm certified only
as $1.5706$- or $4/3$-competitive does not say that it automatically adapts
to these easy cases.

We therefore add a second comparison.  For a multiset $M$, imagine the best
randomized online policy that is told $M$ but not its hidden labelling.  Its
leading cost is an explicit fluid value $n^2\Phi(D_M)$, where $D_M$ is the
empirical distribution.  We ask whether one universal policy, not told $M$,
can attain the same value.  This is an ``outer'' version of online analysis:
on each instance we compare not only with the offline optimum, but also with
every other online policy, including policies given more information.

\begin{theorem}[Randomized instance optimality, informal]
For every fixed common upper bound $u$, there is an explicit benchmark
$\Phi_u(D)$ and one randomized policy, not told the processing-time multiset,
whose cost on every multiset $M\subseteq[0,u]$ is
\[
 n^2\Phi_u(D_M)+o_u(n^2).
\]
No randomized policy---even one told $M$---can improve the leading term under
a uniformly hidden labelling; consequently some fixed oblivious labelling
witnesses the same lower bound.

The analogous statement holds in the blind-execution model for every fixed
bound $p_i\le L$, with a different explicit benchmark
$\Phi_{\rm blind}(D_M)$ and error $o_L(n^2)$.
\end{theorem}

Randomization is essential to the universal part of this statement.  A
deterministic policy has a fixed pattern of labels from which it tries to
learn, and an oblivious labelling can make that pattern unrepresentative.  A
random pilot instead estimates the histogram of any fixed multiset.  Once
the histogram is known approximately, the policy solves a one-dimensional
quadratic optimization and executes the resulting threshold schedule.

The boundedness qualifications in the two parts should not be confused with
the obligatory theorem above.  The blind theorem assumes $p_i\le L$, and the
common-upper theorem already has $p_i\le u$.  They use these bounds to make
the pilot and grid errors uniformly $o(n^2)$.  Randomized obligatory testing,
by contrast, allows arbitrary processing times and uses its cutoff/fallback
argument instead.

\subsection{The information view: blind execution}

[proc je vysledek z tehle sekce pridany k vysledku z predchozi? jsem z toho uplne zmateny. ]

We now drop the obligation to test.  An untouched job may be run blindly for
its actual duration $p_i$; only after it finishes is that duration known.
Thus a test does not make the job faster.  It buys the option to postpone a
long outcome and to move a short outcome forward.

Here a worst-case ratio to the clairvoyant optimum can be infinite.  A
zero-heavy multiset illustrates the obstruction.  The optimum puts all zero
jobs first at no processing cost, whereas an online policy cannot identify
those jobs without either testing many labels or occasionally running a
positive job before many hidden zeros.  As the positive fraction tends to
zero, the online leading cost can exceed the clairvoyant leading cost by an
arbitrarily large factor.  Instance optimality remains meaningful: it asks
for the best cost possible under the same hidden-labelling uncertainty,
rather than dividing by a benchmark that may be subquadratic.

For a distribution $D$, write $\mu=\mathbb E P$ and choose the testing
threshold $\tau$ from
\[
 \mathbb E(\tau-P)^+=1.
\]
Testing one unit of job mass and immediately completing outcomes below
$\tau$ behaves like one divisible completion module.  For any fixed tested
fraction $q$, all remaining work is optimally arranged by increasing cost per
completion.  The resulting canonical policy is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \text{threshold tests}
 &\longrightarrow \text{known values below $\mu$}
 \longrightarrow \text{blind jobs}\\
 &\longrightarrow \text{known values above $\mu$}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Its normalized cost $F_D(q)$ is quadratic in $q$, and
$\Phi_{\rm blind}(D)=\min_{0\le q\le1}F_D(q)$.  If the test module is no
denser than blind execution, the minimizer is $q=0$ and the policy simply
runs every job blindly.  Otherwise a genuine interior stopping fraction can
be optimal.  A three-point example in \cref{sec:blind} shows that the known
middle block cannot in general be omitted.

The universal policy learns this distributional schedule from a sublinear
blind pilot.  Conversely, a completion-envelope argument rules out any
better leading cost, even for an adaptive policy that knows the multiset.
This is the blind part of the preceding informal theorem; its precise
statement is \cref{thm:blind-instance-intro}.

\subsection{The optimization view: a common upper bound}

In the second interpretation, raw execution of every untouched job takes a
known common time $u$.  A unit test reveals an optimized time $p_i\le u$ and
allows the scheduler to use it.  Small $u$ makes optimization unattractive;
large $u$ makes raw execution expensive.  Letting $u$ grow recovers
obligatory testing, but the finite values are not a monotone interpolation:
at intermediate $u$, the offline optimum can exploit raw execution in ways
that are particularly difficult to imitate online.

\begin{theorem}[Common-upper curves, informal]
For every fixed $u>0$, the exact size-asymptotic deterministic competitive
ratio is a continuous six-piece function $V(u)$.  Its global maximum is
$1.8667603992\ldots$, and for every $u$ beyond
$4.5052\ldots$ it is already equal to the obligatory value
$1.5705740966\ldots$.

Against an oblivious adversary, the exact randomized ratio is a continuous
four-piece function $R_{\rm rand}(u)$.  Its global maximum is
$1.6257523846\ldots$, and it is exactly $4/3$ for every $u\ge25/4$.
\end{theorem}

The deterministic curve is supported by three algorithmic shapes.  For
small $u$, run every job raw.  For intermediate $u$, test jobs and combine a
forced immediate prefix with a fixed short/long threshold.  For large $u$,
use the history-dependent obligatory threshold, with a temporary reserve
that pays for capped outcomes.  The matching adversaries move through binary
zero/cap instances, capped versions of the harmonic construction, and
finally the cap-free harmonic instance.  These changes of optimal policy and
hard instance create the six branches.

The randomized result is best understood through the stronger benchmark
$\Phi_u$.  For a fixed empirical distribution, the optimal leading policy
tests a fraction $q$, immediately completes outcomes below its density
threshold, processes the tested residual stock in SPT order, and finally
runs untouched jobs raw.  Dividing its value by the clairvoyant coefficient
$\Omega_u(D)$ gives the instance-specific ratio
\[
 \alpha_u(D)=\frac{\Phi_u(D)}{\Omega_u(D)}.
\]
The exact oblivious curve is the outer maximization
\[
 R_{\rm rand}(u)=\sup_D\alpha_u(D).
\]
This infinite-dimensional optimization collapses to binary distributions.
Different zero/cap mixtures give the two middle branches, and the balanced
$\{0,2\}$ distribution gives the final $4/3$ plateau.  Thus the curve is not
merely a worst-case calculation: it is the envelope of a per-multiset
instance-optimal theorem.  Exact formulas are given in
\cref{thm:optional-intro,thm:random-common-upper-intro}.

\subsection{Techniques}

Four ideas recur across the models.

\paragraph{Pairwise delay accounting.}
An operation contributes to the completion time of its own job and once
more for every other job still unfinished.  The objective is therefore a sum
of diagonal terms and one delay term per pair of jobs.  The diagonal is only
$O(n)$ after normalization, while the $\Theta(n^2)$ pair terms determine the
exact asymptotic constants.  Deterministic lower bounds make many pairs wait
in the wrong order; deterministic upper bounds charge those errors to an
offline pair through a bank potential.

\paragraph{Completion density and envelopes.}
A test together with the outcomes completed immediately can be viewed as a
module: it spends some work and completes some job mass.  Maximizing mass per
unit work yields a threshold.  Once these modules, known processing classes,
and raw or blind jobs are divisible, their best order is fractional
knapsack.  The area below the unfinished-mass curve gives both the explicit
quadratics $\Phi_u$ and $\Phi_{\rm blind}$ and a lower bound against arbitrary
adaptive stopping rules.

\paragraph{Random relabelling and learning.}
The algorithm privately permutes labels and uses an $o(n)$ pilot to estimate
the empirical distribution.  For lower bounds, first-touch causality gives a
complementary fact: the adaptive order in which a deterministic policy first
touches uniformly labelled occurrences is itself a uniform permutation.
The test/raw decision is predictable from the earlier revealed values.  A
single without-replacement concentration event can therefore control every
prefix, after which the realized stopping fraction is inserted pathwise into
the fluid envelope.

\paragraph{From instance values to exact curves.}
For randomized common-upper scheduling we first solve every empirical
distribution, obtaining $\Phi_u(D)$.  Only then do we maximize
$\Phi_u(D)/\Omega_u(D)$.  Survival-function flattening reduces the outer
optimization to scalar binary families.  This separation between the inner
algorithmic problem and the outer worst-case distribution is what makes the
four-piece curve transparent.

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

Scheduling with tests is part of optimization with explorable uncertainty,
where uncertain data can be queried at a cost; the query viewpoint goes back
at least to Kahan~\cite{Kahan1991}.  Levi, Magnanti, and Shaposhnik study a
stochastic scheduling model in which tests diagnose random job attributes
and derive prefix-testing policies followed by expected-Smith-ratio
order~\cite{LeviMagnantiShaposhnik2019,LeviMagnantiShaposhnik2024}.  Our
four-block blind policy is a unit-weight fluid analogue, but our multiset is
adversarial, need not converge, is not announced to the universal policy,
and is hidden by an oblivious labelling.

The adversarial common-upper model was introduced by Dürr et
al.~\cite{DurrErlebachMegowMeissner2020}.  For one machine and total
completion time they obtained deterministic bounds, including the
$1.8668\ldots$ UTE guarantee for binary values under a common upper bound.
Our deterministic theorem closes the common-upper gap for arbitrary
$p_i\in[0,u]$ and every fixed $u$.  Our randomized theorem gives a separate
exact four-piece curve against an oblivious adversary.  Related variants
allow nonuniform test times~\cite{AlbersEckl2021,LiuLiuWongZhang2023,
KrekelbergEtAl2026}.  Other work considers testing
budgets~\cite{DameriusEtAl2023}, multiple
machines~\cite{GongChenHayashi2024}, or weights~\cite{BuldSchulz2026}.

Dogeas, Erlebach, and Liang introduced obligatory-test scheduling for total
completion time~\cite{DogeasErlebachLiang2024}.  For uniform tests they gave
the SIDLE upper bound $1.58451\ldots$ and the deterministic lower bound
$\sqrt2$.  Liang and Liang subsequently raised the single-machine lower
bound to $3/2$ through a completion-threshold framework
~\cite{LiangLiang2026}.  Our harmonic construction and adaptive threshold
meet at $1.5705740966\ldots$, while our randomized result resolves the
oblivious single-machine value at $4/3$.

The processing-time-oracle model of Dufoss{\'e} et
al.~\cite{DufosseEtAl2022} is close to blind execution: an untested job takes
its true duration, but only two possible durations are allowed.  We permit an
arbitrary bounded multiset and obtain a distribution-sensitive benchmark and
four-block policy.  If no tests are available and processing times are
revealed only at completion, one obtains the classical nonclairvoyant model
of Motwani, Phillips, and Torng~\cite{MotwaniPhillipsTorng1994}.  These
semantics differ from common-upper raw execution, which always lasts the
declared upper bound, regardless of the hidden optimized value.

The rest of the paper first fixes the models and offline benchmarks in
\cref{sec:model}.  It then proves the deterministic obligatory endpoint and
finite common-upper curve, the randomized common-upper and obligatory
results, and the blind instance-optimal theorem.  The machine-checked scope
is summarized in \cref{sec:formalization}.

\subsection{The exact answers}

The obligatory endpoint had a gap between the best known deterministic lower
and upper bounds \cite{DogeasErlebachLiang2024,LiangLiang2026}.  The finite
common-upper problem likewise had no exact answer for arbitrary revealed
values \cite{DurrErlebachMegowMeissner2020}.  We close both gaps in the
natural size-asymptotic convention.

Let $\zstar>1$ be the unique solution of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:zstar}
 \zstar-3=\log\zstar,
\end{equation}
where all logarithms are natural, and put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:Rstar}
 \Rstar=1+\frac{2}{\zstar-1}
       =1.570574096649395\ldots,
 \qquad
 \zstar=4.505241495792883\ldots .
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Obligatory endpoint]\label{thm:main-intro}
The following matching statements hold for $u=\infty$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textup{(\roman*)}]
\item For every deterministic online algorithm $\mathcal B$ and every
      $\eps>0$, there are arbitrarily large instances $I$ for which
      \[
        \ALG_{\mathcal B}(I)\ge(\Rstar-\eps)\OPT(I).
      \]
\item There is one deterministic algorithm $\mathcal A$ and one absolute
      constant $C$ such that every $n$-job instance satisfies
      \[
        \ALG_{\mathcal A}(I)\le\Rstar\OPT(I)+Cn.
      \]
\end{enumerate}
Consequently the optimal deterministic size-asymptotic value at $u=\infty$
is exactly $\Rstar$.
\end{theorem}

For the finite curve, let $\varphi=(1+\sqrt5)/2$ and define
\[
 \udiam=\frac{1+\sqrt{3+2\sqrt5}}2,
 \qquad
 \uzero=1+s_0,\quad s_0^3=(s_0+1)^2,
\]
where $s_0>1$.  For $s=u-1$, let $\rho_I(u)$ be the positive root of
\[
 s\rho^2+(s^3+s^2+1)\rho-(s^2+s+1)(s+2)=0.
\]
On $\varphi+2\le u\le\zstar$, define $\Cmix(u)=1+c$ by the unique pair
$(c,m)$ satisfying
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-mixed-system}
 \artanh m-m
 =1-\frac1c+\frac12\log\!\left(1+\frac2c\right),
 \qquad
 u=1+\frac2c-m,
\end{equation}
with $2/(\zstar-1)\le c\le1/\varphi$ and $0\le m\le1/\varphi$.

\begin{theorem}[Finite common-upper curve]
\label{thm:optional-intro}
For every fixed $u>0$, the optimal deterministic size-asymptotic competitive
ratio is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:intro-optional-curve}
 \V(u)=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&0<u\le1,\\
  u,&1\le u\le\udiam,\\
  \rho_I(u),&\udiam\le u\le\uzero,\\
  1+1/\sqrt{u-1},&\uzero\le u\le\varphi+2,\\
  \Cmix(u),&\varphi+2\le u\le\zstar,\\
  \Rstar,&u\ge\zstar.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}
Every lower branch holds against arbitrary deterministic algorithms on
arbitrarily large fixed instances.  Every upper branch is attained up to
$O_u(n)$.  On the finite part of the last branch, one algorithm and one
absolute $O(n)$ constant work simultaneously for all $u\ge\zstar$.
Together with \cref{thm:main-intro}, the same witness covers the endpoint
$u=\infty$.
\end{theorem}

The deterministic theorem is only the first part of the landscape.  Against
an oblivious adversary, private randomization gives the following exact
four-piece curve.  Let $\urand>1$ be the unique root of
\begin{equation}
 \urand^3-6\urand^2+5\urand-1=0,
 \qquad
 \urand=5.048917339522306\ldots .
 \label{eq:intro-random-transition}
\end{equation}

\begin{theorem}[Randomized finite common-upper curve]
\label{thm:random-common-upper-intro}
For every fixed $u>0$, the optimal randomized size-asymptotic competitive
ratio in the common-upper model against an oblivious adversary is
\begin{equation}
 \Rrand(u)=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&0<u\le1,\\[2pt]
  \displaystyle\frac{u^3}{u^2+(u-1)^3},
    &1\le u\le\urand,\\[8pt]
  \displaystyle 1+\frac{1}{2(\sqrt u-1)},
    &\urand\le u\le25/4,\\[8pt]
  \displaystyle\frac43,&u\ge25/4.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:intro-random-common-upper-curve}
\end{equation}
More precisely, there are randomized nonanticipating algorithms
$\mathcal A_{n,u}$ and numbers $\eps_u(n)\to0$ such that every fixed labelled
vector $p\in[0,u]^n$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \EE\ALG_{\mathcal A_{n,u}}(p)
 \le \Rrand(u)\OPT_u(p)+\eps_u(n)n^2.
 \label{eq:intro-random-common-upper}
\end{equation}
Conversely, for every randomized online algorithm and every $\eps>0$, there
are arbitrarily large $n$ and a fixed vector $p\in[0,u]^n$ for which
\begin{equation}
 \EE\ALG(p)\ge\bigl(\Rrand(u)-\eps\bigr)\OPT_u(p).
 \label{eq:intro-random-common-lower}
\end{equation}
The curve is continuous at all three junctions.  Its global maximum is
attained at $u=(3+\sqrt3)/2$ and equals
\begin{equation}
 \max_{u>0}\Rrand(u)
 =\frac{27+6\sqrt3}{23}
 =1.625752384583185\ldots .
 \label{eq:intro-random-common-maximum}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

Thus randomization improves every nontrivial part of the deterministic
finite-cap curve.  The last branch already has value $4/3$, but passing to
$u=\infty$ still requires a proof uniform over unbounded processing times.
That endpoint remains sharp even when the processing-time multiset is not
announced.

\begin{theorem}[Randomized obligatory testing]
\label{thm:random-obligatory-intro}
There is a universal randomized nonanticipating algorithm, depending only on
$n$, such that every fixed vector $p\in[0,\infty)^n$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \EE\ALG(p)
 \le \frac43\OPT(p)+20378n^{7/4}
 \le\left(\frac43+40756n^{-1/4}\right)\OPT(p).
 \label{eq:intro-random-upper}
\end{equation}
Conversely, for every randomized algorithm and every sufficiently large even
$n$, some fixed labelling of $n/2$ zero jobs and $n/2$ jobs of processing
time two satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \EE\ALG(p)\ge n^2-O(n^{3/2}),
 \qquad
 \OPT(p)=\frac34n^2+n.
 \label{eq:intro-random-lower}
\end{equation}
Hence the exact randomized size-asymptotic competitive ratio for obligatory
testing against an oblivious adversary is $4/3$.
\end{theorem}

Our third result uses a stronger, instance-dependent comparison.  In the
blind-execution model, sparse zero-heavy instances may have clairvoyant cost
$o(n^2)$, so an additive $o(n^2)$ theorem is not a conventional competitive
ratio.  It instead says that the algorithm learns, at negligible leading
cost, everything that advance knowledge of the multiset could contribute to
the oblivious minimax value.

\begin{theorem}[Blind-execution instance optimality]
\label{thm:blind-instance-intro}
Fix $L<\infty$.  There are a randomized nonanticipating policy $\mathcal A^*$,
which knows $n,L$ but not the processing-time multiset, and a deterministic
sequence $\eps_L(n)\to0$ with the following properties.  For every multiset
$M\subseteq[0,L]$ of size $n$ and every fixed labelling $\sigma$,
\begin{equation}
 \EE C_{\mathcal A^*}(\sigma(M))
 \le n^2\Phiopt(D_M)+\eps_L(n)n^2.
 \label{eq:intro-blind-upper}
\end{equation}
For every randomized policy $\mathcal A$, even one told $M$, some fixed
oblivious labelling $\sigma$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \EE C_{\mathcal A}(\sigma(M))
 \ge n^2\Phiopt(D_M)-\eps_L(n)n^2.
 \label{eq:intro-blind-lower}
\end{equation}
The benchmark $\Phiopt$, defined in \cref{sec:blind-benchmark}, is the minimum
of an explicit one-variable quadratic.  One may take
$\eps_L(n)=O_L(n^{-1/6}\sqrt{\log(n+2)})$ analytically; the formal proof also
provides a slower explicit vanishing rate using only finite second-moment
estimates.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Why total completion time leads to pair accounting}

The most useful way to think about total completion time is pairwise.  Every
operation of job $i$ contributes once to $C_i$, and it contributes once more
for each other job that remains unfinished while that operation is executed.
Equivalently, the objective consists of diagonal job terms and one delay term
for every unordered pair of jobs.  The offline pair term is simply the
shorter effective length.  The online pair term records whether an earlier
revealed job was processed immediately or left waiting.

This representation explains both sides of our proof.  A lower-bound
adversary tries to make many pairs wait in the wrong order.  An upper-bound
algorithm tries to ensure that every expensive local decision can be charged
to a corresponding offline pair.  Since there are $\Theta(n^2)$ pairs but
only $n$ diagonal terms, controlling the quadratic pair coefficient gives
the exact size-asymptotic ratio; bounded rounding errors of $O(1)$ per job
become only $O(n)$.

\subsection{Lower-bound intuition: hide the short jobs until the end}

At the obligatory endpoint, the adversary reveals equal blocks of positive
jobs from longer to shorter values and reveals a large zero block last.  The
algorithm is forced to choose between two risks.  Processing a revealed job
early may delay many still-hidden zeros.  Deferring it may leave a large tail
that delays everything completed later.  The adaptive revelation is frozen
after the interaction, so a standard replay argument produces one fixed
instance for the chosen deterministic algorithm.

The processing times are separated by harmonic gaps.  This choice is not a
guess about the final constant.  The zero mass and the number of shorter
positive blocks give two multiplicities; their sum exactly cancels the
denominator of each harmonic gap.  After this cancellation, every possible
postponement made by the algorithm has a nonnegative coefficient and every
own-phase choice contributes the same completed square.  All algorithmic
decisions disappear from the leading term.  What remains is a one-variable
ratio describing the relative positive and zero masses, and its stationary
equation is \eqref{eq:zstar}.

For finite $u$, two additional adversarial pictures appear.  When $u$ is
small or moderate, binary values $p\in\{0,u\}$ already suffice: the adversary
watches how much work the algorithm completes before revealing whether the
next jobs are zeros or caps.  For the transition to the obligatory value,
the hard instance is a capped prefix followed by a scaled copy of the
harmonic core.  The prefix exploits the optimum's ability to skip tests; the
core exploits uncertainty in the test order.  As $u$ grows, the optimal cap
mass decreases continuously to zero, leaving exactly the obligatory
construction.

\subsection{Upper-bound intuition: three policies and one common bank}
\label{sec:intro-upper-intuition}

The exact upper curve uses three simple algorithmic shapes.  Their formal
pseudocode appears later; here we describe why they are natural.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.24\linewidth}p{0.68\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
approximate range & upper-bound policy at a glance \\
\midrule
$u\lesssim1.87$
 & Do not test: execute every job raw. \\
$1.87\lesssim u\lesssim3.15$
 & Test all jobs; always finish an initial fraction, then use a short/long
   threshold.  The initial fraction shrinks as $u$ grows. \\
$3.15\lesssim u\lesssim3.62$
 & Test all jobs; finish values at most one immediately and sort the rest in
   a final tail. \\
$3.62\lesssim u\lesssim4.51$
 & Use the history-dependent threshold; the proof adds a reserve for capped
   outcomes. \\
$u\gtrsim4.51$, including $\infty$
 & Use the same adaptive threshold, now with no cap reserve. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\paragraph{Raw policy.}
When $u$ is very small, information costs at least as much as it can save.
The algorithm therefore runs every job raw.  Slightly beyond that trivial
range, raw execution need no longer be clairvoyantly optimal for each job,
but comparing its length $u$ with the optimum's effective lengths already
gives the first nontrivial minimax branch.

\paragraph{Forced-prefix threshold policy.}
In the next regimes the algorithm tests every job.  During an initial
fraction of the test order it processes every revealed value immediately.
After that prefix it processes only sufficiently short outcomes and sends
the rest to a shortest-processing-time tail.  The forced prefix guarantees
that some jobs finish before too many tests accumulate; the suffix threshold
protects the schedule from long revealed jobs.  As $u$ increases, the useful
prefix shrinks continuously to zero.  The algebraic branch is exactly the
point where the worst binary mixture is tangent to the target ratio; after
the prefix vanishes, balancing a capped tail against a threshold-one tail
gives the square-root branch.

\paragraph{Adaptive-threshold policy.}
For large $u$, a fixed prefix is too rigid.  The algorithm again tests jobs
in a fixed order, but its immediate-processing threshold reacts to the
observed history.  It remembers only the number $N$ of positive outcomes and
the number $D$ of those outcomes that were deferred.  A normalized balance
of the form
\[
 \frac{D-(1+c)N}{\text{number of untested jobs}}
\]
measures how much future delay has already been promised to the tail.  Near a
neutral balance the algorithm can afford to process moderately long jobs
immediately.  Every positive outcome drives the balance downward, while a
deferral refunds one unit because future tests will also delay that tail job.
When the normalized balance becomes sufficiently negative, the threshold
falls toward one.  All deferred jobs are sorted only after the tests finish.

The analysis first fixes the symbolic immediate/deferred path.  Convexity
then reduces every ordinary revealed value to one of four endpoints: a true
zero, a positive value tending to zero, the immediate side of the live
threshold, or its deferred side.  A nonnegative bank potential pays the
excess pair cost at each endpoint.  The logarithmic threshold is forced by
making the immediate and deferred boundary equations balance simultaneously;
the obligatory constant is forced by calibrating the bank's initial value.

Finite $u$ creates exactly one new endpoint: a capped value $p=u$, for which
the optimum can use raw execution and avoid the test.  On the middle bridge
this endpoint can temporarily overdraw the ordinary bank, so we add a reserve
whose balance depends only on the observed cap fraction.  At the end of the
bridge the reserve becomes zero.  With $r=\Rstar-1$, the worst residual
charge of the cap endpoint is at most $2-r(u-1)$.
The right-hand side is nonpositive precisely when
$u\ge1+2/r=\zstar$.  Thus beyond $\zstar$ the obligatory four-endpoint bank
already pays for the fifth endpoint.  The plateau in
\cref{eq:intro-plateau} is a proof-level fact, not merely a numerical
coincidence.

\subsection{Randomized intuition: completion density and sampling}

The randomized common-upper and obligatory results have a different core.
Suppose for a moment that the multiset is announced.  Choose a
processing-time prefix $E$, test jobs in a uniformly random order, process a
revealed job immediately exactly when it lies in $E$, and sort the remaining
tail after the last test.  The expected cost of this stationary policy has a
closed pairwise formula.  The right prefix maximizes
\[
 \frac{\text{mass completed during discovery}}
      {\text{test work plus immediate processing work}}.
\]
Writing the reciprocal of this density as $\theta$, the optimizer is a
threshold: early values are at most $\theta$ and late values are at least
$\theta$.

For a finite cap, the algorithm compares this stationary policy with running
every job raw.  Writing $H(t)=\Pr(P>t)$ and $s=u-1$, the offline quadratic
coefficient is
\[
 \frac12\left(1+\int_0^s H(t)^2\,dt\right).
\]
The density threshold satisfies
$\int_0^\theta(1-H(t))\,dt=1$.  Splitting according to whether
$\theta\ge s$ or $\theta\le s$ reduces the worst distribution to one of two
binary endpoints.  The distributions on $\{0,u\}$ give the two middle
branches of \eqref{eq:intro-random-common-upper-curve}; the balanced
$\{0,2\}$ distribution gives the $4/3$ plateau.  The first transition occurs
when the stationary critical point meets the raw/stationary crossing, which
is exactly the cubic in \eqref{eq:intro-random-transition}; the second occurs
at $u=25/4$.

At the obligatory endpoint, a short algebraic identity writes
$4\OPT-3\EE\ALG$ as a sum of three nonnegative terms.  Equality again
identifies the hard distribution: half of the mass at zero and half at two.

For a finite cap, bounded support makes the announcement easy to remove: a
pilot of size $n^{2/3}$ selects between raw execution and the stationary
prefix on a grid of $n^{1/3}$ cells, losing only $O_u(n^{11/6})$.  At the
unbounded obligatory endpoint, the algorithm instead tests $n^{3/4}$
uniformly random jobs and learns the threshold on a grid of $n^{1/4}$ cells.
Closing the chosen sample prefix under its numerical threshold is essential:
otherwise a cell missed by the sample could violate the population
separation.  Values above a fixed cutoff are always deferred.  If the learned
completion density is too small, the algorithm falls back to
test-all-then-SPT; precisely in that case the offline objective is already
large enough to pay for the fallback.  This gives the upper bound in
\cref{eq:intro-random-upper}.  For the lower bound, a uniformly labelled
half-zero, half-two multiset is a balanced random bridge.  At every time, the
number of completed jobs is at most one half of the work spent, up to the
bridge discrepancy.  Integrating this envelope gives
\cref{eq:intro-random-lower}.

\subsection{Blind execution: from tests to a fractional knapsack}

For a processing-time distribution $D$, let $\mu=\EE P$ and define $\tau$ by
\[
 \EE(\tau-P)^+=1.
\]
A unit mass of tests followed by immediate processing of outcomes below
$\tau$ completes mass $a=\Pr(P<\tau)$ using work
$1+\EE[P\one_{P<\tau}]=a\tau$.  It is therefore a divisible completion
module of work $\tau$ per completed job.  Untested blind jobs form a second
module of cost $\mu$ per completion; tested residual values form ordinary
known SPT classes.

For any fixed final tested fraction $q$, a completion-envelope relaxation
turns these objects into a fractional knapsack.  Its greedy order is
\[
 \begin{aligned}
 \text{test module}&\ \longrightarrow\
   \text{known residual values below $\mu$}\ \longrightarrow\
   \text{blind block}\\[-2pt]
 &\hspace{10em}\longrightarrow\
   \text{known residual values above $\mu$}.
 \end{aligned}
\]
The area below the corresponding unfinished-mass curve is an explicit
quadratic $F_D(q)$; minimizing it defines $\Phiopt(D)$.  This pointwise
envelope, rather than a local exchange in an adaptive decision tree, is what
makes the lower bound robust.

The remaining issue is to compare a completely adaptive finite policy with
that deterministic fluid picture.  Distinguish equal-valued occurrences and
map each hidden labelling to the order in which the policy first touches
them.  Prefix causality makes this reveal map a bijection of the finite
permutation space.  Hence a uniformly labelled input becomes an ordinary
without-replacement urn and the test/blind decision at the next touch is
predictable from the preceding revealed values.  One simultaneous urn event
then controls every prefix; only after fixing that event do we substitute the
policy's realized stopping fraction $q$.  Rounding to a growing grid and a
sublinear blind pilot complete the transfer to an arbitrary bounded,
unannounced multiset.

\subsection{Why the infinite endpoint needs a uniform proof}

For each fixed processing-time vector, the finite-$u$ offline lengths
$q_u(p)$ eventually equal $1+p$.  Nevertheless, a competitive-ratio proof
cannot blindly send $u$ to infinity: the worst instance may choose
$p\approx u$, the algorithm may depend on $u$, and the hidden constant in an
$O_u(n)$ estimate may diverge.  We avoid every such interchange.  The lower
bound uses one bounded harmonic core, which embeds in all sufficiently large
finite caps.  The high-$u$ upper bound uses one $u$-independent adaptive
algorithm and one absolute linear remainder.  Consequently the endpoint
$u=\infty$ and the finite plateau are certified by the same witnesses.

The linear remainder is lower order because
$\OPT\ge n(n+1)/2$ at $u=\infty$ and, for fixed $u$, the analogous finite
bound is also quadratic.  It implies an endpoint coefficient
$\Rstar+\eps$ with a fixed additive constant for every $\eps>0$.  We do
\emph{not} prove the stronger statement
$\ALG\le\Rstar\OPT+B$ with one fixed $B$, nor the unrelaxed inequality
$\ALG\le\Rstar\OPT$ on every finite instance.

\subsection{How to read the proofs}

After the literature review and common framework, \cref{sec:obligatory}
begins the deterministic part with the cap-free endpoint.  Its lower
subsection gives the harmonic
adversary and a reusable scaled-core lemma.  Its upper subsection formally
states the adaptive algorithm and proves the ordinary four-endpoint bank.
These are matching and may still be read independently.

\Cref{sec:optional} then treats finite caps.  The binary stopping game and
the two simple policies prove the first four branches.  The capped-prefix
lower construction reuses the harmonic core, while the middle/high upper
bound reuses the ordinary bank and analyzes only the new cap endpoint and
its reserve.  At $u=\zstar$ both cap corrections vanish.
\Cref{app:finite-checkpoint,app:cap-bubbling} contain an exact rational
checkpoint for the cap-free lower construction and an independent
cap-bubbling certificate.

\Cref{sec:random-common-upper} proves the exact randomized finite-cap curve.
It first identifies the announced stationary benchmark, reduces the
distributional maximization to a scalar binary game, then removes announcement
by a sublinear random pilot and proves the matching oblivious Yao bounds.
\Cref{sec:random-obligatory} proves the sharp randomized $4/3$ endpoint
theorem uniformly for unbounded processing times.  It first solves the
announced stationary problem, then learns the threshold from a sample, and
finally gives the balanced binary Yao lower bound.
\Cref{sec:blind} turns to blind execution.  It defines the fluid benchmark,
proves the fixed-$q$ envelope theorem, transfers the envelope to arbitrary

\Cref{sec:formalization} records the machine-checked scope; detailed

\cref{app:lean-formalization}.

\section{Models, benchmarks, and common preliminaries}\label{sec:model}

There are $n$ jobs, all available at time zero, and one machine.  Operations
are nonpreemptive, and at most one operation can run at a time.  A unit test
of job $j$ reveals its processing time $p_j\ge0$.

We use the following three operation models throughout.
\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[Common-upper execution $\mathsf{CU}(u)$.]
For finite $u$, all jobs share the known upper limit $p_j\in[0,u]$.  A job is
completed either by raw execution for exactly $u$ time, or by a unit test and
subsequent processing for $p_j$.  Raw execution reveals no hidden value.

\item[Obligatory testing $\mathsf{OT}$.]
Processing times are arbitrary finite nonnegative numbers.  Every job must be
tested for one unit and subsequently processed for $p_j$; the processing may
be delayed after the test.  This is also the formal endpoint
$\mathsf{CU}(\infty)$.

\item[Blind execution $\mathsf{BE}(L)$.]
Here $p_j\in[0,L]$.  A job may be run blindly for its actual duration $p_j$,
at whose completion its duration becomes known, or tested for one unit and
subsequently processed for $p_j$.  Thus blind execution never pays an upper
bound in place of the actual processing time.
\end{description}

The completion time $C_j$ is the completion time of the processing operation;
in tested mode, when $p_j=0$, this is the instant immediately after the test.
In the blind model, a blind zero job completes immediately when selected.
The objective is $\sum_j C_j$.  The number $n$ and the parameter $u$ are
known to a common-upper algorithm; a blind-execution algorithm knows $n,L$.

We write $\ALG_{\mathcal A}(I)$ for the objective value of an online algorithm
$\mathcal A$ on an instance $I$, and $\OPT(I)$ for the value of an offline
optimum that knows all processing times.  We omit subscripts and arguments
when they are clear.

\subsection{Information and adversaries}

The deterministic common-upper results allow the usual adversarial input and
use adaptive revelation only as a lower-bound device that is frozen by
\cref{lem:replay}.  The randomized results use an \emph{oblivious} adversary:
the labelled processing-time vector is fixed before the algorithm draws its
private random seed.  Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the algorithm is
not told the multiset of values.  An \emph{announced} policy is additionally
given that multiset but not its assignment to labels.  Announcing the
multiset can only help and is therefore a valid strengthening in lower bounds.

All randomized expectations are over private algorithmic randomness.  A
uniform random relabelling is private randomness, not a distributional
assumption on the adversarial multiset.  For lower bounds we use a uniform
distribution over fixed labellings as a finite Yao distribution and then
select one labelling whose cost is at least the average.

\subsection{Offline optima and pair accounting}

Define the clairvoyant effective length by
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optional-effective-length}
 q_u(p)\defeq
 \begin{cases}
  \min\{u,1+p\},&u<\infty,\\
  1+p,&u=\infty.
 \end{cases}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Unified offline order]
\label{lem:offline}\label{lem:optional-offline}
Let $q_i=q_u(p_i)$ and let $q_{(1)}\le\cdots\le q_{(n)}$.  For every
$u\in(0,\infty]$, an offline optimum completes the jobs in nondecreasing
order of $q_i$ and has value
\begin{align}
 \OPT_u
 &=\sum_{k=1}^n\sum_{i=1}^kq_{(i)},
 \label{eq:unified-opt-prefix}\\
 &=\sum_iq_i+\sum_{i<j}\min\{q_i,q_j\}.
 \label{eq:optional-opt-pair}
\end{align}
For finite $u$, it uses raw mode when $u\le1+p_j$ and tested mode otherwise.
At $u=\infty$, it tests every job and processes the jobs in nondecreasing
$p_j$ order.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $C_{[1]}\le\cdots\le C_{[n]}$ be the completion times of any feasible
schedule.  Completing job $j$ consumes at least $q_j$ units: for finite $u$
this is the shorter of its two modes, and for $u=\infty$ it is its compulsory
test--process length.  By time $C_{[k]}$ the machine has completed some $k$
jobs, so
\[
 C_{[k]}\ge\sum_{i=1}^kq_{(i)}.
\]
The schedule that takes the shorter available mode for every job and executes
the resulting blocks in nondecreasing $q_i$ order attains every inequality.
Summing and expanding the prefix sums gives both formulas.
\end{proof}

In the blind-execution model, a clairvoyant optimum never tests: it knows all
values and can execute each job blindly for $p_j$.  The same shortest-first
argument gives
\begin{equation}
 \OPT_{\mathsf{BE}}(p)
 =\sum_i p_i+\sum_{i<j}\min\{p_i,p_j\}.
 \label{eq:blind-offline-opt}
\end{equation}
We will not use this quantity as the principal benchmark of
\cref{thm:blind-instance-intro}: it may be $o(n^2)$, while that theorem
compares policies at the $n^2$ scale to the stronger announced-multiset
online benchmark $\Phiopt(D_M)$.

At the cap-free endpoint, the pair formula specializes to
\begin{align}
 \OPT_\infty
 &=\sum_{k=1}^n\left(k+\sum_{i=1}^kp_{(i)}\right),
 \label{eq:opt-prefix}\\
 &=\sum_i(1+p_i)+\sum_{i<j}\bigl(1+\min\{p_i,p_j\}\bigr).
 \label{eq:opt-pair}
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:opt-n2}
 \OPT_\infty\ge\frac{n(n+1)}2.
\end{equation}
For finite $u$, $q_j\ge\min\{u,1\}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optional-opt-n2}
 \OPT_u\ge\min\{u,1\}\frac{n(n+1)}2.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Competitive-ratio conventions}

The ordinary competitive ratio of $\mathcal A$ is
\[
  \CR(\mathcal A)=\sup_I\frac{\ALG_{\mathcal A}(I)}{\OPT(I)}.
\]
For the result of this paper, the relevant size-asymptotic ratio is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cr-infty}
  \CR_\infty(\mathcal A)
  =\limsup_{n\to\infty}\ \sup_{|I|=n}
       \frac{\ALG_{\mathcal A}(I)}{\OPT(I)}.
\end{equation}
The additive-constant convention calls a coefficient $c$ admissible for
$\mathcal A$ if there exists $B<\infty$ such that
\[
  \ALG_{\mathcal A}(I)\le c\OPT(I)+B
  \qquad\text{for every }I.
\]
We write
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cr-add}
  \CR_{\rm add}(\mathcal A)
  =\inf\{c:\text{$c$ is admissible for $\mathcal A$}\}.
\end{equation}
The optimal deterministic values in the two conventions are obtained by
taking the infimum of $\CR_\infty(\mathcal A)$ and
$\CR_{\rm add}(\mathcal A)$, respectively, over deterministic algorithms.
For any fixed $u$, an estimate $\ALG\le c\OPT+O_u(n)$ fixes
\cref{eq:cr-infty} at most $c$ and, by
\cref{eq:opt-n2,eq:optional-opt-n2}, makes every coefficient $c+\eps$
admissible.  It need not make the endpoint coefficient $c$ admissible.

\begin{remark}[No hidden interchange of limits]\label{rem:no-limit-swap}
The notation $u=\infty$ identifies one model exactly.  We never infer an
endpoint upper bound merely by taking $u\to\infty$ in an estimate whose
algorithm or additive term may depend on $u$.  The endpoint estimate is
proved directly in \cref{sec:upper}; \cref{sec:optional-upper} then verifies
that the same algorithm and certificate work uniformly for every finite
$u\ge\zstar$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Test-order adversaries}

The lower bound will assign processing times according to the order in which
tests finish.  This is only a proof device.

\begin{lemma}[Replay]\label{lem:replay}
Fix a deterministic online algorithm.  Suppose all untested jobs have the
same known parameters, and an adversary assigns processing times
deterministically from the transcript and test-completion order.  The
resulting interaction is reproduced by a fixed input instance.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Run the interaction once and record the value revealed for every tested job
label.  Assign those values to the corresponding labels in advance.  An
induction over operation completions shows that the deterministic algorithm
chooses the same tests, processing operations, and idle intervals on the fixed
instance.  If the algorithm fails to test or complete some job forever, its
competitive ratio is already infinite; otherwise every relevant label is
recorded.
\end{proof}

\section{The cap-free endpoint \texorpdfstring{$u=\infty$}{u=infinity}}
\label{sec:obligatory}

\begin{corollary}[Exact asymptotic values]\label{cor:asymptotic}
The optimal deterministic size-asymptotic value and the optimal deterministic
additive-constant infimum both equal $\Rstar$.
Moreover, for every $\eps>0$, the Adaptive-Threshold algorithm satisfies
\begin{equation}\label{eq:additive-eps}
  \ALG(I)\le(\Rstar+\eps)\OPT(I)+B_\eps
\end{equation}
for all instances, with $B_\eps$ independent of $I$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The upper bound and \cref{eq:opt-n2} imply
\[
  \frac{\ALG}{\OPT}
  \le\Rstar+\frac{2C}{n+1},
\]
so the size-asymptotic ratio is at most $\Rstar$.  The lower bound is
\cref{thm:lower}.  For the additive statement, Young's inequality and
\cref{eq:opt-n2} give
\[
  Cn\le\frac\eps2n^2+\frac{C^2}{2\eps}
      \le\eps\OPT+\frac{C^2}{2\eps}.
\]
Thus one can take $B_\eps=C^2/(2\eps)$, proving that every coefficient above
$\Rstar$ is admissible for this algorithm.  Conversely, fix $c<\Rstar$.
By \cref{thm:lower}, every deterministic algorithm has a sequence of instances
with $\OPT\to\infty$ and $\ALG/\OPT>c+\delta$ for some $\delta>0$.  The
inequality $\ALG\le c\OPT+B$ would then force
$\delta\le B/\OPT\to0$, a contradiction.  This proves the additive-constant
claim.
\end{proof}

\section{Discussion and open questions}\label{sec:discussion}

The two common-upper curves identify the leading quadratic constants of the
deterministic and oblivious-randomized games for every finite $u$; the
deterministic analysis also includes $u=\infty$.  Their scope is deliberately
precise: one machine, all release dates zero, unit tests, nonpreemptive
operations, total completion time, and one common upper limit when the cap is
finite.  The deterministic upper proof loses at most a constant on each of
the $n$ discrete transitions.  This loss is asymptotically negligible, but
it is not a fixed additive constant over the whole instance.  On
$u\in[\zstar,\infty]$ its per-transition constant is uniform in $u$.

For finite $u$, the randomized curve is learned with an
$O_u(n^{11/6})$ remainder.  This exponent is only a convenient consequence
of the pilot/grid parameters; sharper empirical-process estimates should
reduce it without changing the four leading branches.  The randomized proof
uses the oblivious assumption essentially.  Its random-labelling lower bound
does not apply to an adversary that observes the private choices, so the
adaptive-adversary curve remains open.

The randomized obligatory theorem changes both the algorithmic power and the
adversary: the input is fixed obliviously, while the algorithm may privately
permute labels.  Its $4/3$ upper bound is uniform over unbounded processing
times and unknown multisets.  The additive $O(n^{7/4})$ term is unlikely to be
optimal.  The announced stationary theorem suggests that sharper empirical-
process estimates, avoiding the coarse histogram and sample-first schedule,
could reduce it substantially without changing the leading constant.

The blind-execution theorem has a different benchmark.  It is
instance-optimal up to $o(n^2)$ relative to the announced oblivious minimax
value, but it is not a bounded competitive-ratio theorem relative to the
clairvoyant SPT optimum in \cref{eq:blind-offline-opt}.  This distinction is
unavoidable for the present statement: on sparse instances the clairvoyant
cost can be subquadratic and the permitted error may dominate it.  Natural
next questions are whether a finer, instance-dependent error can recover a
meaningful competitive guarantee on those sparse inputs, and whether the
fixed bounded-support assumption can be replaced by a moment condition.

\begin{conjecture}[Endpoint additive competitiveness]\label{conj:endpoint}
There exist a deterministic online algorithm and a constant $B<\infty$ such
that every instance at the cap-free endpoint satisfies
\[
  \ALG(I)\le \Rstar\OPT(I)+B.
\]
\end{conjecture}

The present Adaptive-Threshold algorithm is the natural first candidate.
In \cref{eq:upper-exact-I,eq:upper-exact-Q} the exact local contributions
contain favorable negative terms that are intentionally discarded before the
bank-account calculation.  A discrete potential that retains those terms
might prove \cref{conj:endpoint}; alternatively, a finite-instance
obstruction might show that a different policy is needed.  The current lower
bound does not distinguish these possibilities.  In particular, our result
neither proves nor claims the stronger ordinary inequality with \(B=0\).

The analogous endpoint-additive question is open for each nontrivial
finite-cap deterministic branch as well: our claims concern $\CR_\infty$ and
the infimum of admissible additive coefficients.  Also open are
adaptive-adversary randomization, heterogeneous upper limits, nonuniform test
times in the exact-ratio regime, weights, and more than one machine.  The
deterministic common-upper curve shows why a direct extension is unlikely to
have one universal threshold: already in the uniform case the optimal policy
changes form at five analytically distinct junctions.

The lower construction also suggests a structural principle for future
lower bounds: hard instances reveal many decreasing positive levels, with
continuum increment \(dp=ds/(1+s)\), and postpone zero jobs to the end.  Our
argument proves that this pattern is sufficient.  It remains open whether
every asymptotically extremal construction must have this form.

\section{Conclusion}

Uniform tests produce three different but related models.  For deterministic
common-upper execution, the exact answer is the six-piece curve
\cref{eq:intro-optional-curve}; its cap-free endpoint is
$\Rstar=1.570574096649395\ldots$ and its global maximum is
$\udiam=1.866760399173862\ldots$.  The proof combines binary stopping games,
a harmonic adversary, and adaptive bank potentials.

Against an oblivious adversary, randomization gives the exact four-piece
finite-cap curve \cref{eq:intro-random-common-upper-curve}.  Comparing raw
execution with a maximum-density stationary threshold, and flattening the
survival function, reduces its upper bound to two scalar binary families.  A
sublinear pilot learns the right family without knowing the multiset, while
binary random labellings give all matching lower branches.  At the obligatory
endpoint, a uniform unbounded-support argument learns the stationary
threshold without any boundedness assumption, and the balanced half-zero,
half-two instance proves that $4/3$ remains sharp.

When untested jobs instead run for their actual duration, the right answer is
instance dependent.  Tests, known residual classes, and blind work become
divisible completion modules; their fractional-knapsack order yields the
four-block policy and the quadratic benchmark $\Phiopt(D)$.  The adaptive-
reveal bijection and a simultaneous urn bound show that neither adaptive
stopping nor prior knowledge of the multiset improves its leading $n^2$
coefficient.  Together, these results identify completion density and
pairwise delay as the common organizing principles behind deterministic
competitive ratios, randomized learning, and instance-optimal testing.
