\section{Models and common preliminaries}\label{sec:model}

Every setup we consider slightly different. We try to collect common building blocks in this section so that the subsequent sections are less repetitive. Still, some amount of repetition is necessary due to the fact that the models and the optimal algorithms often turn out to be subtly different. 

\paragraph{roadmap}
short roadmap for this section. 

\subsection{Model}

This section makes the four models from the introduction precise and records
the common facts used throughout the paper.  There are $n$ jobs, all
available at time zero, and one machine.  Operations are nonpreemptive, and
at most one operation can run at a time.  Job $j$ has a hidden value
$p_j\ge0$.  The four models differ in what can be done before this value is
known.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*,style=nextline]
\item[Obligatory testing $\mathsf{OT}$.]
The values $p_j$ are arbitrary finite nonnegative numbers.  Every job must
first be tested for one unit.  The test reveals $p_j$, after which its
processing operation of length $p_j$ may be performed immediately or
delayed.

\item[Blind execution $\mathsf{BE}$.]
A job may be run blindly for its actual duration $p_j$;
the value becomes known only when that execution finishes.  Alternatively,
the job may be tested for one unit, revealing $p_j$, and its processing
operation may then be delayed.  Testing provides information but does not
shorten the job.

\item[Blind optimization $\mathsf{BO}(u)$.]
Here $p_j\in[0,u]$.  A job may be run raw for the known time $u$.
Alternatively, one unit of optimization changes its running time from $u$
to $p_j$ without revealing $p_j$; the optimized job is subsequently run
blindly, possibly after a delay.  Its value becomes observable only when that
execution finishes.  Raw execution reveals no hidden value.

\item[Revealing optimization $\mathsf{RO}(u)$.]
Again $p_j\in[0,u]$, and raw execution takes the known time $u$.  The other
option is a unit test that reveals $p_j$, followed, possibly after a delay,
by processing for $p_j$.  Thus the preliminary action provides both the
speedup and its size.
\end{description}

In OT and BE, we sometimes consider L -- the maximum length of a job. The values $n$, $u$, $L$ are known to the algorithm.

The completion time $C_j$ is the time at which job $j$ finishes.  If a test
reveals $p_j=0$, the job completes at the end of that test.  A blind zero job
in $\mathsf{BE}(L)$ completes as soon as it is selected, whereas an optimized
zero job in $\mathsf{BO}(u)$ first pays its unit optimization cost.  The
objective is
\[
                              \sum_{j=1}^n C_j.
\]

Obligatory testing can be viewed as the limit of revealing optimization in
which raw execution becomes unavailable.  We use this only as intuition:
the obligatory theorems and their algorithms are proved directly though, using this argument directly would require us to be very careful about both $u$ and $n$ going to infinity. 

We write $\cost(\mathcal A,I)$ for the objective value of an online algorithm
$\mathcal A$ on an instance $I$, and $\cost(\OPT,I)$ for the value of an
offline optimum that knows all processing times.  In calculations we use the
shorter abbreviations
\[
 \ALG_{\mathcal A}(I)=\cost(\mathcal A,I),
 \qquad
 \OPT(I)=\cost(\OPT,I),
\]
and omit algorithms or instances when they are clear.  Thus $\OPT$ denotes
an algorithm inside $\cost(\OPT,I)$ and its cost only in the abbreviated
notation $\OPT(I)$.

\subsection{Information and adversaries}

The deterministic curve results allow the usual adversarial input and use
adaptive revelation only as a lower-bound device that is frozen by
\cref{lem:replay}.  The randomized results use an \emph{oblivious} adversary:
the labeled processing-time vector is fixed before the algorithm draws its
private random seed.  Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the algorithm is
not told the multiset of values.  An \emph{announced} policy is additionally
given that multiset but not its assignment to labels.  Announcing the
multiset can only help and is therefore a valid strengthening in lower bounds.

All randomized expectations are over private algorithmic randomness.  A
uniform random relabeling is private randomness, not a distributional
assumption on the adversarial multiset.

\begin{remark}[Order-oblivious convention]
\label{rem:order-oblivious}
For a multiset $M$ of $n$ occurrences and a randomized policy $\mathcal A$,
write
\[
 \cost^{\mathrm{oo}}(\mathcal A,M)
 \defeq \max_{\sigma\in\mathfrak S_n}
       \EE\cost(\mathcal A,\sigma(M)).
\]
This is the standard order-oblivious view of instance optimality: the input
is a multiset and its order carries no instance information
~\cite{AfshaniBarbayChan2017,NarayananRozhonTetekThorup2024}.
In the present models the convention is especially harmless.  Given any
policy $\mathcal A$, let $\mathcal A^{\mathrm{sym}}$ first draw a uniform
permutation $\Pi$ of the public labels and then simulate $\mathcal A$ through
that relabeling.  For every fixed labeling $\sigma$,
\[
 \EE\cost(\mathcal A^{\mathrm{sym}},\sigma(M))
 =\EE_{\Pi}\EE\cost(\mathcal A,\Pi\sigma(M)),
\]
which is independent of $\sigma$ and at most
$\cost^{\mathrm{oo}}(\mathcal A,M)$.  Hence randomized policies may be
symmetrized without loss, after which only the multiset matters.  When the
introduction compares costs ``on $M$,'' it uses this convention.  An
announced policy knows $M$, but not its assignment to the public labels.
\end{remark}

For lower bounds we use a uniform distribution over fixed labelings as a
finite Yao distribution and then select one labeling whose cost is at least
the average, as formalized in \cref{lem:finite-yao} below.

\subsection{Offline optima and pair accounting}

The offline optimum knows all values $p_j$.  It therefore chooses the shorter
available way to complete each job and orders the resulting job blocks
shortest first.  For later use, define
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optional-effective-length}
 \lambda_u(p)\defeq\min\{u,1+p\}
 \qquad(0<u<\infty),
\end{equation}
and associate with job $j$ the model-dependent \emph{effective length}
\begin{equation}
 \lambda_j\defeq
 \begin{cases}
  1+p_j, & \mathsf{OT},\\
  p_j, & \mathsf{BE}(L),\\
  \lambda_u(p_j), & \mathsf{BO}(u)\text{ or }\mathsf{RO}(u).
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:model-effective-length}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Effective lengths and the offline pair identity]
\label{lem:offline}\label{lem:optional-offline}
Fix any one of the four models and let
$\lambda_{(1)}\le\cdots\le\lambda_{(n)}$ be its effective lengths from
\cref{eq:model-effective-length}.  A clairvoyant optimum completes the jobs
in this order and has value
\begin{align}
 \OPT
 &=\sum_{k=1}^n\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_{(i)},
 \label{eq:unified-opt-prefix}\\
 &=\sum_i\lambda_i+\sum_{i<j}\min\{\lambda_i,\lambda_j\},
 \label{eq:optional-opt-pair}\\
 &=\frac12\left(
      \sum_i\lambda_i+\sum_{i,j}\min\{\lambda_i,\lambda_j\}
    \right).
 \label{eq:unified-opt-symmetric}
\end{align}
In $\mathsf{OT}$ it tests every job.  In $\mathsf{BE}(L)$ it runs every job
blindly.  In $\mathsf{BO}(u)$ and $\mathsf{RO}(u)$ it uses raw mode when
$u\le1+p_j$ and the unit preliminary action followed by processing otherwise.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $C_{[1]}\le\cdots\le C_{[n]}$ be the ordered completion times of any
feasible schedule.  Completing job $j$ consumes at least $\lambda_j$ units of work
belonging to that job.  Hence, by time $C_{[k]}$, the machine has performed
the required work of some $k$ jobs, and
\[
 C_{[k]}\ge\sum_{i=1}^k\lambda_{(i)}.
\]
Choose the mode specified in the lemma, keep each resulting block contiguous,
and execute the blocks in nondecreasing order of $\lambda_j$.  This schedule is
feasible in the relevant model and attains equality for every $k$.  Summing
over $k$ and then collecting the contribution of each unordered pair gives
the first two formulas.  Splitting the double sum into its diagonal and the
two orientations of every unordered pair gives the symmetric form.
\end{proof}

For any finite input object $I$ whose occurrences have processing times
$p_1,\ldots,p_n$, write
\begin{equation}
 D[I]\defeq\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\delta_{p_i}.
 \label{eq:empirical-distributions}
\end{equation}
Thus $D[M]$ and $D[p]$ are instances of the same empirical-law operator;
labels matter to the online interaction, but not to $D[I]$.

For a probability distribution $D$ and a nonnegative effective-length map
$\ell$, let $P,P'$ be independent with law $D$ and define
\begin{equation}
 \Omega_\ell(D)\defeq\frac12\EE\min\{\ell(P),\ell(P')\}.
 \label{eq:common-offline-coefficient}
\end{equation}
We use the model-specific abbreviations
\begin{align}
 \Omega_{\mathsf{OT}}(D)
  &\defeq\frac12\EE\min\{1+P,1+P'\},
 &\Omega_{\mathsf{BE}}(D)
  &\defeq\frac12\EE\min\{P,P'\},\notag\\
 \Omega_{\mathsf{BO},u}(D)=\Omega_{\mathsf{RO},u}(D)
  &\defeq\frac12\EE\min\{\lambda_u(P),\lambda_u(P')\}.
 \label{eq:model-offline-coefficients}
\end{align}
The last coefficient is common to blind and revealing optimization; their
online benchmarks differ because only a revealing test exposes $p_j$ before
execution.

\begin{corollary}[Empirical effective-length identity]
\label{cor:empirical-offline}
Let $\Lambda,\Lambda'$ be independent uniform draws from the multiset of
effective lengths $\{\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n\}$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \OPT=\frac{n^2}{2}\EE\min\{\Lambda,\Lambda'\}
      +\frac n2\EE\Lambda.
 \label{eq:empirical-offline}
\end{equation}
For a multiset $M$ and the effective-length map $\ell$ of the chosen model,
\begin{equation}
 \OPT(M)=n^2\Omega_\ell(D[M])
       +\frac n2\int\ell(p)\,dD[M](p).
 \label{eq:empirical-offline-model}
\end{equation}
In particular, in $\mathsf{BO}(u)$ or $\mathsf{RO}(u)$,
\begin{equation}
 \OPT_u(M)=n^2\Omega_{\mathsf{RO},u}(D[M])
       +\frac n2\int\lambda_u(p)\,dD[M](p).
 \label{eq:empirical-offline-u}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Interpret the two sums in \cref{eq:unified-opt-symmetric} as expectations
under independent uniform draws from the occurrences.  Substituting
$\lambda_j=\ell(p_j)$ gives \cref{eq:empirical-offline-model}; the capped
specialization uses $\ell=\lambda_u$.
\end{proof}

The revealing-optimization and blind-optimization models have the same offline
effective lengths.  Their difference is entirely online: a test reveals
$p_j$, whereas optimization does not.  Blind execution is different even
offline, because a clairvoyant scheduler can run a job for $p_j$ without
paying the unit test.  Thus
\begin{equation}
 \OPT_{\mathsf{BE}}(M)
 =n^2\Omega_{\mathsf{BE}}(D[M])+\frac n2\int p\,dD[M](p)
 =\sum_i p_i+\sum_{i<j}\min\{p_i,p_j\}.
 \label{eq:blind-offline-opt}
\end{equation}
This is not the principal benchmark in \cref{thm:blind-instance}.  The
clairvoyant value may be $o(n^2)$, whereas that theorem compares policies at
the $n^2$ scale with the optimal announced-multiset online value
$\PhiBE(D[M])$.

For obligatory testing, \cref{eq:unified-opt-prefix,eq:optional-opt-pair}
specialize to
\begin{align}
 \OPT_{\mathsf{OT}}
 &=\sum_{k=1}^n\left(k+\sum_{i=1}^kp_{(i)}\right),
 \label{eq:opt-prefix}\\
 &=\sum_i(1+p_i)+\sum_{i<j}\bigl(1+\min\{p_i,p_j\}\bigr).
 \label{eq:opt-pair}
\end{align}
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:opt-n2}
 \OPT_{\mathsf{OT}}\ge\frac{n(n+1)}2.
\end{equation}
For $\mathsf{BO}(u)$ and $\mathsf{RO}(u)$, $\lambda_j\ge\min\{u,1\}$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:optional-opt-n2}
 \OPT_u\ge\min\{u,1\}\frac{n(n+1)}2.
\end{equation}
There is deliberately no analogous positive lower bound for blind execution:
an input consisting only of zero jobs has clairvoyant cost zero.

We also isolate the random-order identity used by several revealing
arguments.  For $p=(p_1,\ldots,p_n)$, a set $E\subseteq[n]$ is a
\emph{processing-time prefix} if every value in $E$ is at most every value
outside it.  Suppose that $E$ contains all zero jobs, put $T=[n]\setminus E$,
and use the normalized quantities
\begin{equation}
 e=|E|,\quad a=\frac en,\quad h=1-a,\quad
 \ell_E=\frac1n\sum_{i\in E}p_i,\quad
 K_X=\frac1{n^2}\sum_{i,j\in X}\min\{p_i,p_j\}.
 \label{eq:stationary-prefix-notation}
\end{equation}
The prefix property gives
\begin{equation}
 K(D[p])=K_E+2h\ell_E+K_T,
 \qquad
 K(D)\defeq\iint\min\{x,y\}\,dD(x)dD(y).
 \label{eq:stationary-kernel-split}
\end{equation}
Define its stationary leading coefficient by
\begin{equation}
 F_{\mathsf{OT},D[p]}(E)
 = (1+\ell_E)\left(1-\frac a2\right)+\frac{K_T}{2}.
 \label{eq:stationary-leading-coefficient}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Stationary prefix pair identity]
\label{lem:stationary-prefix-cost}
Test all jobs in a uniformly random order, process a positive $E$-job
immediately after its test, defer every $T$-job, and finally process $T$ in
SPT order.  The resulting stationary policy satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \EE\ALG_{\Stat}(p,E)
 =n^2F_{\mathsf{OT},D[p]}(E)+\frac{e+\sum_i p_i}{2}.
 \label{eq:stationary-prefix-cost}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $W=n+\sum_{i\in E}p_i$ and
$W_E=e+\sum_{i\in E}p_i$.  The discovery phase has one block per job: an
$E$-block has length $1+p_i$ and completes its job, whereas a $T$-block is
its unit test and completes no job.  For an $E$-job, every other block
precedes it with probability $1/2$, so all $E$-jobs contribute
\begin{equation}
 \frac{eW+W_E}{2}.
 \label{eq:stationary-early-cost}
\end{equation}
Every $T$-job sees the common offset $W$, and the final SPT phase contributes
\begin{equation}
 (n-e)W+\sum_{i\in T}p_i
 +\sum_{\substack{i<j\\i,j\in T}}\min\{p_i,p_j\}.
 \label{eq:stationary-late-cost}
\end{equation}
Adding the two displays and collecting the diagonal terms gives
\cref{eq:stationary-prefix-cost}.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Maximum-density modules and fluid completion envelopes}
\label{sec:common-fluid-envelopes}

Several randomized and instance-optimal arguments use the same fluid
primitive.  Testing one unit mass of jobs automatically completes its zero
outcomes and may be followed by immediate processing of selected positive
outcomes.  We isolate that primitive, its optimal density, and the resulting
fractional-knapsack envelope here.

Let $D$ be a probability distribution on $[0,\infty)$ with finite mean and
let $P\sim D$.  For a measurable selector $g(p)\in[0,1]$ on the positive
values, define its completed mass and work by
\begin{equation}
 a_D(g)\defeq D(\{0\})+\EE[g(P)\one_{P>0}],
 \qquad
 w_D(g)\defeq 1+\EE[P g(P)\one_{P>0}].
 \label{eq:common-module-aw}
\end{equation}
The zero mass is included automatically because its test completes it.

\begin{lemma}[Maximum-density discovery module]
\label{lem:maximum-density-module}
There is a unique $\tau_D\ge1$ satisfying
\begin{equation}
                         \EE(\tau_D-P)^+=1.
 \label{eq:common-threshold-equation}
\end{equation}
Choose $g_*$ to select every positive $p<\tau_D$, reject every $p>\tau_D$,
and select an arbitrary fraction of an atom at $p=\tau_D$.  Put
\begin{equation}
 a_*=a_D(g_*),\qquad w_*=w_D(g_*),\qquad
 d\nu(p)=(1-g_*(p))\one_{p>0}\,dD(p),
 \label{eq:common-module-data}
\end{equation}
with $\nu(\{0\})=0$.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{a_*}{w_*}=\max_g\frac{a_D(g)}{w_D(g)}=\frac1{\tau_D},
 \qquad
 \tau_D a_D(g)\le w_D(g)\quad\text{for every }g.
 \label{eq:common-density-dual}
\end{equation}
Moreover, $\nu$ has mass $1-a_*$ and is supported on $[\tau_D,\infty)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The function $h(t)=\EE(t-P)^+$ is continuous and nondecreasing, satisfies
$h(0)=0$, and tends to infinity because $h(t)\ge t-\EE P$.  Once positive it
is strictly increasing, so $h(t)=1$ has a unique solution; $h(t)\le t$ gives
$\tau_D\ge1$.

For an arbitrary selector $g$,
\[
 \tau_D a_D(g)-\EE[P g(P)\one_{P>0}]
 =\tau_D D(\{0\})+\EE[(\tau_D-P)g(P)\one_{P>0}]
 \le\EE(\tau_D-P)^+=1.
\]
This is the dual inequality in \cref{eq:common-density-dual}.  The threshold
selector $g_*$ attains equality because it keeps exactly the terms with
$\tau_D-p>0$; mass at $p=\tau_D$ does not affect the equality.  Hence
$\tau_D a_*=w_*$ and $g_*$ has maximum density $1/\tau_D$.  Its unselected
mass is $1-a_*$ and can occur only at values at least $\tau_D$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Empirical maximum-density prefix]
\label{cor:empirical-density-prefix}
For an empirical distribution $D[p]$ from
\cref{eq:empirical-distributions}, the maximum of
\begin{equation}
 \frac{|E|/n}{1+n^{-1}\sum_{i\in E}p_i}
 \label{eq:empirical-prefix-density}
\end{equation}
over processing-time prefixes $E$ containing all zero jobs is
$1/\tau_{D[p]}$.  It is attained by taking every occurrence below
$\tau_{D[p]}$, no occurrence above it, and any subset of occurrences equal to
$\tau_{D[p]}$.  Thus every selected value is at most $\tau_{D[p]}$ and every
unselected value is at least $\tau_{D[p]}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Apply \cref{lem:maximum-density-module} to $D[p]$.  At the boundary
$p_i=\tau_{D[p]}$, adding or removing an occurrence preserves the ratio
$1/\tau_{D[p]}$, so the fractional boundary selector may be replaced by any
subset of the tied occurrences.
\end{proof}

We will also use the continuous analogue of shortest-processing-time pair
accounting.  Let $\mathcal M=\sum_k c_k\delta_{v_k}$ be a finitely supported
probability measure on $[0,\infty)$.  Its divisible completion envelope is
\begin{equation}
 K_{\mathcal M}(x)\defeq
 \max\left\{\sum_k z_k:
       0\le z_k\le c_k,\ \sum_kv_kz_k\le x\right\}.
 \label{eq:divisible-spt-envelope}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Divisible SPT area]
\label{lem:divisible-spt-area}
The envelope $K_{\mathcal M}$ processes the items in nondecreasing order of
$v_k$, and
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^\infty\bigl(1-K_{\mathcal M}(x)\bigr)\,dx
 =\frac12\iint\min\{r,s\}\,d\mathcal M(r)d\mathcal M(s).
 \label{eq:divisible-spt-area}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The ordering assertion is the usual fractional-knapsack exchange.  In a
block of completion mass $c_k$ and work per completion $v_k$, unfinished
mass decreases linearly.  After sorting the blocks by $v_k$, its area
contribution is
\[
 v_kc_k\left(1-\sum_{h<k}c_h-\frac{c_k}{2}\right).
\]
Summing these terms and collecting each unordered pair gives
\cref{eq:divisible-spt-area}.
\end{proof}

Assume from now on that $D$ has finite support and write
$d_p=D(\{p\})$.  Fix a final tested fraction $q\in[0,1]$ and an independent
completion mode that uses $v\ge0$ work per completed job.  At a fluid
schedule prefix, let $t$ be tested mass, $b$ mass completed by the independent
mode, and $c_p$ processed tested mass of positive class $p$.  Define
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C^v_{D,q}(x)\defeq
 \max\left\{
 d_0t+b+\sum_{p>0}c_p:
 \begin{array}{l}
 0\le t\le q,\quad 0\le b\le1-q,\\
 0\le c_p\le d_pt,\\
 t+vb+\sum_{p>0}pc_p\le x
 \end{array}\right\}.
 \label{eq:common-fluid-envelope}
\end{equation}

\begin{lemma}[Test-module completion envelope]
\label{lem:test-module-envelope}
Let $(a_*,\tau_D,\nu)$ be supplied by
\cref{lem:maximum-density-module}.  Then
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C^v_{D,q}=K_{\mathcal M_{D,q,v}},
 \qquad
 \mathcal M_{D,q,v}\defeq
  qa_*\delta_{\tau_D}+q\nu+(1-q)\delta_v.
 \label{eq:common-envelope-items}
\end{equation}
Consequently,
\begin{equation}
 \int_0^\infty\bigl(1-\mathcal C^v_{D,q}(x)\bigr)\,dx
 =\frac12\iint\min\{r,s\}\,
   d\mathcal M_{D,q,v}(r)d\mathcal M_{D,q,v}(s).
 \label{eq:common-envelope-area}
\end{equation}
The three terms in $\mathcal M_{D,q,v}$ are respectively the maximum-density
test module, the residual tested classes, and the independent completion
mode.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Split every positive support class virtually into its $g_*$-selected and
residual parts.  For a feasible point in
\cref{eq:common-fluid-envelope}, let $y_L,x_L$ be the completion and work
contributed by the tests, tested zeros, and processed selected outcomes.
Applying \cref{eq:common-density-dual} to the processed fractions within the
first $t$ units of tests gives
\[
                         \tau_D y_L\le x_L,
 \qquad                  y_L\le a_*t.
\]
With $\lambda=y_L/a_*$, replace this part by $\lambda$ full test modules.
The replacement preserves completed mass, weakly decreases work, and uses
$0\le\lambda\le t\le q$.  Residual class $p$ has capacity at most
$q\nu(\{p\})$, while the independent mode has capacity $1-q$.  Thus every
feasible point maps to the divisible knapsack in
\cref{eq:common-envelope-items}.

Conversely, order the knapsack items by work per completion, placing the test
module before residual items at a tie.  The independent item can be run
whenever its position in this order requires.  A partial test-module item is
realized by the corresponding fraction of tests and selected outcomes.  Once
all $q$ test modules have run, every residual capacity is available; this is
early enough because \cref{lem:maximum-density-module} gives
$\operatorname{supp}\nu\subseteq[\tau_D,\infty)$.  Hence the greedy curve is
feasible at every work budget, proving the envelope identity.  The area
formula follows from \cref{lem:divisible-spt-area}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Zero-preserving transport]
\label{lem:zero-preserving-transport}
Suppose distributions $D,D'$ admit a coupling $(P,P')$ such that
$P=0$ if and only if $P'=0$ and $|P-P'|\le h$ almost surely.  Transporting
the same fluid policy through this coupling preserves all completion events
and changes accumulated processing work by at most $h$.  Consequently, each
model-specific fluid value $\Phi$ defined below satisfies
\begin{equation}
 |\Phi(D)-\Phi(D')|\le h.
 \label{eq:zero-preserving-transport}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Couple each infinitesimal job occurrence and transport decisions using its
coupled mark, exposing that mark only when the model exposes the corresponding
processing time.  Zero preservation makes test completions coincide.  At
every prefix, the total mass that has received processing is at most one, so
replacing its processing times changes cumulative work by at most $h$.  One
unfinished-mass curve is therefore contained in a horizontal $h$-shift of
the other, and its area changes by at most $h$.  Taking the infimum over
policies gives one inequality; exchanging $D,D'$ gives the other.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Competitive-ratio conventions}

Every asymptotic statement in this paper allows a uniform additive
$o(n^2)$ term.  Formally, a policy family $\mathcal A$ has asymptotic
competitive ratio at most $R$ if there is a deterministic sequence
$\eps(n)\to0$ such that every $n$-job instance satisfies
\begin{equation}
 \EE\ALG_{\mathcal A}(I)
 \le R\OPT(I)+\eps(n)n^2.
 \label{eq:cr-infty}
\end{equation}
For a deterministic policy the expectation is omitted.  The optimal ratio
in a model is the infimum of such $R$ over all admissible policy families.

For obligatory testing and for either model with fixed raw time $u>0$,
\cref{eq:opt-n2,eq:optional-opt-n2} show that $\OPT$ is bounded below by a
positive constant times $n^2$.  In these models the definition above is equivalent to
the usual worst-case ratio after taking $n\to\infty$.  This equivalence fails
for blind execution, where $\OPT$ can be $o(n^2)$.  Accordingly,
\cref{thm:blind-instance} makes the absolute statement
$\EE\ALG=n^2\PhiBE(D[M])+o_L(n^2)$ and does not claim a bounded ratio to the
clairvoyant optimum on every sparse input.

\subsection{Test-order adversaries}

The lower bound will assign processing times according to the order in which
tests finish.  This is only a proof device.

\begin{lemma}[Replay]\label{lem:replay}
Fix a deterministic online algorithm.  Suppose all untested jobs have the
same known parameters, and an adversary assigns processing times
deterministically from the transcript and test-completion order.  The
resulting interaction is reproduced by a fixed input instance.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Run the interaction once and record the value revealed for every tested job
label.  Assign those values to the corresponding labels in advance.  An
induction over operation completions shows that the deterministic algorithm
chooses the same tests, processing operations, and idle intervals on the fixed
instance.  If the algorithm fails to test or complete some job forever, its
competitive ratio is already infinite; otherwise every relevant label is
recorded.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Probabilistic tools}
\label{sec:probabilistic-tools}

We collect here the concentration estimates used in the randomized sections.
All of them are finite-space statements.  We use without further mention the
union bound, Markov's inequality
$\Prob(Y\ge t)\le \EE Y/t$ for $Y\ge0$, and the tail identity
$\EE Y=\int_0^\infty\Prob(Y\ge t)\,dt$.

The next deferred-decisions lemma turns adaptive label choices into sampling
without replacement.  Occurrence tokens distinguish equal processing times.

\begin{lemma}[Adaptive first-touch permutation]
\label{lem:adaptive-first-touch}
\label{lem:blind-trace-bijection}
Let $J$ be $n$ public labels, let $\Xi$ be $n$ occurrence tokens, and let a
placement $\sigma:J\to\Xi$ be a bijection.  Fix a deterministic
nonanticipating policy that eventually first-touches every label.  Write
$\ell_1(\sigma),\ldots,\ell_n(\sigma)$ for its labels in order of first
touch and define
\begin{equation}
 R_{\mathcal A}(\sigma)_k=\sigma(\ell_k(\sigma)).
 \label{eq:adaptive-first-touch-map}
\end{equation}
Then $\sigma\mapsto R_{\mathcal A}(\sigma)$ is a bijection from placements
to permutations of $\Xi$.  The action selected at first touch $k$ is a
function of the preceding tokens and is therefore predictable with respect
to the analytically exposed token order.  Consequently, a uniform placement
induces a uniform random permutation of the occurrences.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose two placements induce the same occurrence order.  A lockstep
induction shows that the runs have the same observable history before each
first touch: the preceding tokens and actions are the same, and any
observation made by the model is determined by them.  The deterministic
policy therefore performs the same intervening operations, chooses the same
new label, and selects the same action.  Thus all first-touch labels agree.
Equation~\eqref{eq:adaptive-first-touch-map} then makes the placements equal
on every label.  The map is injective between two sets of size $n!$, hence
bijective.  The same induction stopped before touch $k$ proves
predictability.  Exposing a token value that the policy itself does not see
only enriches the analytical filtration and does not affect the conclusion.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Chernoff bounds]
\label{lem:chernoff}
Let $X=\sum_{i=1}^m X_i$, where the $X_i$ are independent Bernoulli random
variables, and write $\mu=\EE X$.  For every $\delta>0$,
\begin{equation}
 \Prob\bigl(X\ge(1+\delta)\mu\bigr)
 \le \left(\frac{e^\delta}{(1+\delta)^{1+\delta}}\right)^\mu
 \le \exp\!\left(-\frac{\delta^2\mu}{2+\delta}\right).
 \label{eq:chernoff-upper}
\end{equation}
For $0\le\delta\le1$,
\begin{equation}
 \Prob\bigl(X\le(1-\delta)\mu\bigr)
 \le \exp\!\left(-\frac{\delta^2\mu}{2}\right).
 \label{eq:chernoff-lower}
\end{equation}
These are the standard exponential-moment bounds of
Chernoff~\cite{Chernoff1952}.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Sampling without replacement]
\label{lem:sampling-without-replacement}
Let $x_1,\ldots,x_N\in[0,1]$, let $X_1,\ldots,X_N$ be a uniformly random
permutation of these numbers, and put
$\bar x=N^{-1}\sum_i x_i$.  For $1\le k\le N$ and $t\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \Prob\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^kX_i-k\bar x\right|\ge t\right)
 \le 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2t^2}{k}\right).
 \label{eq:without-replacement-hoeffding}
\end{equation}
Moreover, if $\widehat x_k=k^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^kX_i$ and
$\sigma^2=N^{-1}\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2$, then, for $N>1$,
\begin{equation}
 \operatorname{Var}(\widehat x_k)
 =\frac{N-k}{k(N-1)}\sigma^2
 \le\frac{1}{4k}.
 \label{eq:without-replacement-variance}
\end{equation}
If the population is partitioned into $d$ classes with population histogram
$p$ and sample histogram $\widehat p$, the same variance formula and
Cauchy--Schwarz give
\begin{equation}
 \EE\|\widehat p-p\|_1\le\sqrt{\frac d k}.
 \label{eq:without-replacement-histogram}
\end{equation}
Finally, if $H_{k,j}$ counts class $j$ among the first $k$ entries, then
\begin{align}
 \Prob\left(
   \max_{0\le k\le N}\max_{1\le j\le d}|H_{k,j}-kp_j|\ge t
 \right)
 &\le 2d(N+1)\exp\!\left(-\frac{2t^2}{N}\right),
 \label{eq:uniform-prefix-hoeffding}\\
 \EE\max_{0\le k\le N}\max_{1\le j\le d}|H_{k,j}-kp_j|
 &\le C\sqrt{N\ln(2d(N+1))}
 \label{eq:uniform-prefix-mean}
\end{align}
for a universal constant $C$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The estimate \cref{eq:without-replacement-hoeffding} is Hoeffding's
sampling-without-replacement inequality~\cite[Section~6]{Hoeffding1963}.
The variance identity is the elementary finite-population correction.  For a
class of mass $p_j$, it gives
$\EE|\widehat p_j-p_j|\le\sqrt{p_j/k}$; summing and applying
Cauchy--Schwarz proves \cref{eq:without-replacement-histogram}.
Apply \cref{eq:without-replacement-hoeffding} to every binary class indicator
and union-bound over $k$ and $j$ to obtain
\cref{eq:uniform-prefix-hoeffding}.  Integrating that tail bound proves
\cref{eq:uniform-prefix-mean}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Martingale maximal inequalities]
\label{lem:martingale-maximal}
Let $(M_k,\mathcal F_k)_{k=0}^N$ be a real martingale with $M_0=0$.
If it is square integrable, then Doob's inequality gives
\begin{equation}
 \EE\max_{0\le k\le N}|M_k|^2\le4\EE|M_N|^2.
 \label{eq:doob-L2}
\end{equation}
If instead $|M_k-M_{k-1}|\le b_k$ almost surely for deterministic $b_k$,
then maximal Azuma--Hoeffding gives, for every $t\ge0$,
\begin{equation}
 \Prob\left(\max_{0\le k\le N}|M_k|\ge t\right)
 \le2\exp\!\left(-\frac{t^2}{2\sum_{k=1}^N b_k^2}\right).
 \label{eq:maximal-azuma}
\end{equation}
We use the classical forms due to Doob~\cite{Doob1953} and
Azuma~\cite{Azuma1967}; the maximal version of the latter follows by applying
the exponential-supermartingale proof at the first crossing time.
\end{lemma}

\begin{lemma}[Predictable sampling from a random permutation]
\label{lem:predictable-permutation-sampling}
Let $X_1,\ldots,X_N$ be a uniform random permutation of fixed vectors
$x_1,\ldots,x_N\in[0,B]^d$, and let
$\bar x=N^{-1}\sum_i x_i$.  If $A_k\in[0,1]$ is measurable before $X_k$ is
exposed, then for $\delta,\eta\in(0,1)$, with probability at least
$1-\eta$, simultaneously for every coordinate $\ell$ and every
$m\le(1-\delta)N$,
\begin{equation}
 \left|\sum_{k\le m}A_k(X_{k,\ell}-\bar x_\ell)\right|
 \le(1+\delta^{-1})E_{N,d,B,\eta},
 \qquad
 E_{N,d,B,\eta}\defeq
 B\sqrt{2N\ln\!\left(\frac{4d(N+1)}{\eta}\right)}.
 \label{eq:predictable-permutation-sampling}
\end{equation}
The statement remains valid after conditioning on independent private
randomness used to choose the predictable sequence.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For each coordinate, apply
\cref{eq:without-replacement-hoeffding} after scaling by $B$, and union-bound
over coordinates and prefix lengths.  Except with probability at most
$\eta/2$, every centered prefix sum has absolute value at most
$E=E_{N,d,B,\eta}$.  Before draw $k\le(1-\delta)N$, the mean
$\widetilde x_{k-1,\ell}$ of
the remaining urn therefore satisfies
\[
 |\widetilde x_{k-1,\ell}-\bar x_\ell|\le \frac{E}{\delta N}.
\]
For each $\ell$,
\[
 M_{m,\ell}=\sum_{k\le m}A_k(X_{k,\ell}-\widetilde x_{k-1,\ell})
\]
is a martingale with increments bounded by $B$.  The maximal
Azuma--Hoeffding inequality \cref{eq:maximal-azuma}, followed by a union
bound over $\ell$, puts all $|M_{m,\ell}|$ below $E$ except with probability at
most $\eta/2$.  The predictable drift is at most $E/\delta$, which proves
\cref{eq:predictable-permutation-sampling}.  Conditioning and then averaging
proves the final assertion.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Lipschitz empirical minimization]
\label{lem:empirical-minimization}
Let $\Pi$ be a common family of templates and let $F_D(\pi)$ be their
objectives for distributions $D$.  Suppose that, for some metric $d$,
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\pi\in\Pi}|F_D(\pi)-F_E(\pi)|\le Ld(D,E).
 \label{eq:empirical-objective-lipschitz}
\end{equation}
If $\Phi(D)=\min_{\pi\in\Pi}F_D(\pi)$, then
\begin{equation}
 |\Phi(D)-\Phi(E)|\le Ld(D,E).
 \label{eq:empirical-value-lipschitz}
\end{equation}
Moreover, if $\pi_E$ minimizes $F_E$, then
\begin{equation}
 F_D(\pi_E)\le\Phi(D)+2Ld(D,E).
 \label{eq:empirical-minimization-transfer}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Evaluating the $E$-objective at a minimizer for $D$, and conversely, proves
\cref{eq:empirical-value-lipschitz}.  For the second claim, insert
$F_E(\pi_E)=\Phi(E)$ between $F_D(\pi_E)$ and $\Phi(D)$ and apply
\cref{eq:empirical-objective-lipschitz,eq:empirical-value-lipschitz}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Finite Yao selection]
\label{lem:finite-yao}
Let $\nu$ be a finite-support distribution on inputs, and let $g(A,I)$ be an
integrable real payoff.  If
\begin{equation}
 \EE_{I\sim\nu}g(A,I)\ge L
 \label{eq:finite-yao-hypothesis}
\end{equation}
for every deterministic strategy $A$, then for every randomized strategy
$\mathcal A$ there is a fixed $I$ in the support of $\nu$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \EE_{A\sim\mathcal A}g(A,I)\ge L.
 \label{eq:finite-yao-conclusion}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Condition on the random seed of $\mathcal A$ and apply
\cref{eq:finite-yao-hypothesis}.  Averaging over the seed shows that the
$\nu$-average of $\EE_{A\sim\mathcal A}g(A,I)$ is at least $L$, so at least
one input in the finite support attains this average.  This is the one-sided
finite form of Yao's principle~\cite{Yao1977} used in our lower bounds.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Notation and fluid-to-finite transfer}
\label{sec:notation-transfer}

We use one notation hierarchy in all four models.  An uppercase Roman $D$
denotes a probability law, and $D[I]$ is the empirical law
\cref{eq:empirical-distributions} of a finite input object $I$.
Calligraphic uppercase letters denote curves, sets, or policies.  For a
model $\mathsf M$, an input law $D$, and a fluid policy template $\pi$, we
write
\begin{equation}
 \mathcal C_{\mathsf M,D,\pi}(x)
   =\text{maximum completed mass by work }x,
 \qquad
 F_{\mathsf M,D}(\pi)
   =\int_0^\infty\bigl(1-\mathcal C_{\mathsf M,D,\pi}(x)\bigr)\,dx.
 \label{eq:notation-envelope-template}
\end{equation}
Uppercase Greek letters are leading $n^2$-coefficients:
\begin{equation}
 \Omega_{\mathsf M}(D)=\text{offline fluid cost},
 \qquad
 \Phi_{\mathsf M}(D)=\inf_{\pi}F_{\mathsf M,D}(\pi)
   =\text{instance-optimal online value}.
 \label{eq:notation-leading-values}
\end{equation}
Model parameters such as $u$ are appended to the model subscript when
needed.  Finally, $R_{\mathsf M}^{\rm det}$ and
$R_{\mathsf M}^{\rm rand}$ denote worst-case ratios.  We reserve $R$ for a
candidate ratio and $r=R-1$ for its excess.  Lowercase letters denote local
scalars; in particular $a$ is completion mass, $w$ is work, $q$ is a tested
fraction, $\mu$ is a mean, $\kappa$ is completion density, and
$\tau=1/\kappa$ is work per completion.  Specialized formulas may suppress
a template or a fixed model parameter, but retain these semantic roles.

The following reusable results isolate the finite-size argument shared by the
instance-optimal sections.  They deliberately separate the model-specific
task---constructing a fluid envelope and repairing a finite prefix into a
feasible fluid point---from the generic completion-area and learning steps.

\begin{lemma}[Uniform first-touch sampling event]
\label{lem:uniform-first-touch-event}
Fix values $p_1,\ldots,p_n\in[0,L]$, partition their occurrence tokens into
$s+1$ cells with empirical masses $d_0,\ldots,d_s$, and put
$\mu=n^{-1}\sum_i p_i$.  Place the tokens uniformly on the public labels,
fix a deterministic policy, and let $X_k$ be the value encountered at first
touch $k$.  If $A_k$ is the predictable indicator that this touch uses the
revealing mode, then for every $\delta,\eta\in(0,1)$, with probability at
least $1-\eta$, simultaneously for every $m\le(1-\delta)n$,
\begin{align}
 \left|\sum_{k\le m}A_k\one_{X_k\in j}
       -d_j\sum_{k\le m}A_k\right|
 &\le \Gamma_{\rm cell} &&(0\le j\le s),
 \label{eq:first-touch-cell}\\
 \left|\sum_{k\le m}(1-A_k)X_k
       -\mu\sum_{k\le m}(1-A_k)\right|
 &\le \Gamma_{\rm work},
 \label{eq:first-touch-work}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align*}
 \Gamma_{\rm cell}
  &=(1+\delta^{-1})E_{n,s+1,1,\eta/2},\\
 \Gamma_{\rm work}
  &=(1+\delta^{-1})E_{n,1,L,\eta/2}.
\end{align*}
The statement remains true after conditioning on an independent policy seed.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:adaptive-first-touch}, the first-touch values form a uniform
permutation and $A_k$ is predictable.  Apply
\cref{lem:predictable-permutation-sampling} to the cell-indicator vector with
selector $A_k$ and to the scalar value with selector $1-A_k$, assigning
failure probability $\eta/2$ to each application.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Bounded suffix edit]
\label{lem:bounded-suffix-edit}
Consider two completing no-idling schedules on the same $n$ jobs, each of
total work at most $Wn$.  Suppose one is obtained from the other by changing
the operations of at most $h$ jobs, altering at most $ch$ operations of
individual length at most $W$, while preserving the relative order and
completion events of all unaffected jobs.  Then
\begin{equation}
 |\cost(\mathcal A)-\cost(\widetilde{\mathcal A})|
 \le(c+1)Whn.
 \label{eq:bounded-suffix-edit}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write completion cost as operation length times the number of unfinished
jobs.  Altered operations contribute at most $chWn$.  At every unaffected
operation, the unfinished counts in the two schedules differ only on the
$h$ affected jobs; summing this discrepancy over total work at most $Wn$
adds at most $hWn$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Fluid-to-finite completion transfer]
\label{thm:completion-curve-transfer}
Fix a law $D$ and a family of templates $\Pi$.  Suppose every envelope
$\mathcal C_{D,\pi}$ is nondecreasing, takes values in $[0,1]$, and equals
one for $x\ge H$, where $H<\infty$ is common to $\pi\in\Pi$.  Put
\begin{equation}
 F_D(\pi)=\int_0^\infty(1-\mathcal C_{D,\pi}(x))\,dx,
 \qquad \Phi(D)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}F_D(\pi),
 \label{eq:transfer-fluid-values}
\end{equation}
and assume $0\le\Phi(D)\le B$.

Let $\mathcal A$ be a random finite schedule on $n$ jobs.  Suppose that on an
event $G$ there are an edited completing schedule $\widetilde{\mathcal A}$
and a possibly random template $\pi\in\Pi$ such that
\begin{align}
 \cost(\mathcal A)&\ge
   \cost(\widetilde{\mathcal A})-\varepsilon n^2,
 \label{eq:transfer-edit-cost}\\
 \frac{N_{\widetilde{\mathcal A}}(nx)}n
 &\le \mathcal C_{D,\pi}(x+\alpha)+\beta
 \qquad(0\le x\le H),
 \label{eq:transfer-completion-bound}
\end{align}
where $N_{\widetilde{\mathcal A}}(t)$ is the number of jobs completed by
time $t$.  If $\Prob(G^c)\le\eta$, then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\EE\cost(\mathcal A)}{n^2}
 \ge \Phi(D)-\varepsilon-\alpha-H\beta-\eta B.
 \label{eq:completion-curve-transfer}
\end{equation}
The same conclusion holds seedwise under a uniform finite placement; in that
case, for each randomized policy, finite Yao selection produces one fixed
oblivious labeling satisfying the bound.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Total completion time is the area under the unfinished-job curve.  On $G$,
\cref{eq:transfer-completion-bound} and the fact that the envelope is one
after $H$ give
\begin{align*}
 \frac{\cost(\widetilde{\mathcal A})}{n^2}
 &\ge\int_0^H
   \bigl(1-\mathcal C_{D,\pi}(x+\alpha)-\beta\bigr)\,dx\\
 &\ge F_D(\pi)-\alpha-H\beta
 \ge\Phi(D)-\alpha-H\beta.
\end{align*}
Use \cref{eq:transfer-edit-cost}, average over $G$, and use nonnegativity on
$G^c$.  The lost factor is at most $\eta\Phi(D)\le\eta B$, proving
\cref{eq:completion-curve-transfer}.  The final assertion is
\cref{lem:finite-yao} applied after conditioning on the policy seed.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Cell-capacity repair]
\label{lem:cell-capacity-repair}
Let $D$ have support $0=p_0<p_1<\cdots<p_s$ with masses $d_j$, and consider
the common envelope $\mathcal C^v_{D,q}$ from
\cref{eq:common-fluid-envelope}.  At a finite schedule prefix, let $t,b,z$
be normalized tested mass, independent-mode mass, and zero completions, and
let $c_j$ be normalized processed mass of positive class $j$.  Suppose
\begin{equation}
 t\le q,\quad b\le1-q,\quad
 z\le d_0t+\gamma,\quad c_j\le d_jt+\gamma,
 \label{eq:cell-repair-capacities}
\end{equation}
and, with $c_j'=(c_j-\gamma)^+$,
\begin{equation}
 t+vb+\sum_{j=1}^sp_jc_j'\le x+\alpha.
 \label{eq:cell-repair-work}
\end{equation}
Then
\begin{equation}
 z+b+\sum_{j=1}^sc_j
 \le \mathcal C^v_{D,q}(x+\alpha)+(s+1)\gamma.
 \label{eq:cell-capacity-repair}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The repaired variables $t,b,c_j'$ satisfy every constraint in
\cref{eq:common-fluid-envelope} at work budget $x+\alpha$.  Replacing $z$ by
$d_0t$ and each $c_j$ by $c_j'$ loses at most $(s+1)\gamma$ completed mass.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Pilot-learning transfer]
\label{lem:pilot-learning-transfer}
Let $\Pi$ be a common template family, suppose
$\Phi(D)=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi}F_D(\pi)$, and assume
\begin{equation}
 \sup_{\pi\in\Pi}|F_D(\pi)-F_E(\pi)|\le L_0d(D,E).
 \label{eq:pilot-transfer-lipschitz}
\end{equation}
Let $\widehat D$ be a pilot estimate, $D^{\rm rem}$ the law of the remaining jobs,
and let $\widehat\pi$ minimize $F_{\widehat D}$.  If the complete learned
schedule, including its pilot, satisfies conditionally
\begin{equation}
 \EE[\cost(\mathcal A)\mid\widehat D,D^{\rm rem}]
 \le n^2F_{D^{\rm rem}}(\widehat\pi)+\varepsilon n^2,
 \label{eq:pilot-transfer-finite-cost}
\end{equation}
then
\begin{equation}
 \frac{\EE\cost(\mathcal A)}{n^2}
 \le \Phi(D)+2L_0\EE d(\widehat D,D^{\rm rem})
       +L_0\EE d(D^{\rm rem},D)+\varepsilon.
 \label{eq:pilot-learning-transfer}
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{lem:empirical-minimization},
$F_{D^{\rm rem}}(\widehat\pi)\le
\Phi(D^{\rm rem})+2L_0d(\widehat D,D^{\rm rem})$.  The value part of the
same lemma gives
$\Phi(D^{\rm rem})\le\Phi(D)+L_0d(D^{\rm rem},D)$.  Substitute both estimates in
\cref{eq:pilot-transfer-finite-cost} and average.
\end{proof}
