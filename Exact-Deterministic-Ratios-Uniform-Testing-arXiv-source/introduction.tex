\section{Introduction}

Suppose that we have many jobs waiting for one machine.  We want to minimize
the sum of the times at which they finish.  The length of each job is written
on a folded piece of paper.  If we knew all the lengths, the right schedule
would be clear: we would simply run the jobs from shortest to longest.  But
the papers are folded, and opening one of them takes one unit of machine time.

Should we open another paper, or should we start working?

Opening a paper may reveal a tiny job that we can finish immediately.  It may
also reveal a huge job that is best left for the end.  On the other hand,
looking has a cost.  If one hundred jobs are still unfinished, one unit spent
opening a paper adds one hundred units to the sum of completion times.  The
machine must therefore balance two uses of its time: learning which jobs are
short and completing jobs that are already understood.

Variants of this question have appeared in both stochastic and adversarial
scheduling with testing
~\cite{LeviMagnantiShaposhnik2019,DurrErlebachMegowMeissner2020,DogeasErlebachLiang2024}.
We start with its cleanest version, in which every paper has to be opened.
We then allow a job to be run without opening its paper.  Depending on what
this means, we obtain three further variants.  The same simple
ideas---random sampling, thresholding, and shortest-first execution---give
exact answers in all of them.

\subsection{Warmup: every job has to be tested}
\label{sec:intro-obligatory}

There are $n$ jobs.  Testing job $i$ takes one unit of time and reveals a
number $p_i\geq0$.  The job then needs another $p_i$ units of processing; if
$p_i=0$, it completes with the test.  For now every job has to be tested:
testing is \emph{obligatory}.  The formal model is given in
\cref{sec:model}.

We compare an online scheduler with the shortest-first schedule that knows
all values $p_i$ in advance.  The \emph{competitive ratio} is the online cost
divided by this offline optimum.  We care about its asymptotic value, so
lower-order additive terms of size $o(n^2)$ do not matter.

The obligatory-testing model for total completion time was introduced by
Dogeas, Erlebach, and Liang~\cite{DogeasErlebachLiang2024}.  Their work and a
later lower bound of Liang and Liang~\cite{LiangLiang2026} left a gap between
the best deterministic upper and lower bounds.  They also left the randomized
problem open.  We close both gaps.

\begin{theorem}[Obligatory testing, informal version of
\protect\cref{thm:det-obligatory,thm:rand-obligatory}]
\label{thm:intro-obligatory}
In the obligatory-testing model, the best deterministic asymptotic
competitive ratio is $\RdetOT=1.570574\ldots$.  Against an adversary
that fixes the input before the algorithm draws its random bits, the best
randomized ratio is $\RrandOT=4/3$.  The two optimal policies are sketched in
\cref{alg:intro-obligatory-det,alg:intro-obligatory-rand}.
\end{theorem}

Here and below, \emph{shortest first} means processing revealed jobs in
nondecreasing order of $p_i$.  The following pseudocode gives a little more
detail about the two policies.  The formulas are not meant to be memorable;
the shape of the algorithms is.

\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.485\textwidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Deterministic threshold}
\label{alg:intro-obligatory-det}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Put $c=0.570574\ldots$ and $R=1+c=\RdetOT$.
\State Before test $i+1$, where $0\le i<n$, let $c_i$ be the number of
positive jobs processed immediately and $d_i$ the number deferred.
\State Put
\[
 y_i=\frac{d_i-R(c_i+d_i)}{n-i}.
\]
\State Use threshold $T_i=1$ if $y_i\le-1$; otherwise use
\[
 T_i=1+\frac12\ln\frac{c+2}{c-2y_i}.
\]
\State Test the next job.  Process it now if $p\le T_i$; otherwise defer it.
\State After all tests, finish deferred jobs shortest first.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.485\textwidth}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Learned randomized threshold}
\label{alg:intro-obligatory-rand}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Fix $B=32$.  Shuffle the jobs and test $o(n)$ of them to estimate the
distribution of the job length $X$.
\State Treat this estimate as exact.  For each threshold $0\le t\le B$ put
\[
 a(t)=\Pr(X\le t),\qquad
 m(t)=\mathbb E[X\mathbf 1_{X\le t}].
\]
\[
 \widehat\rho=\max_t\frac{a(t)}{1+m(t)}.
\]
\State If $\widehat\rho<2/B$, test every
remaining job and then finish all revealed jobs shortest first.
\State Otherwise set $T=1/\widehat\rho$.  Process the sampled jobs with
$p\le T$.  Keep testing jobs, processing them immediately when $p\le T$ and
deferring them otherwise.
\State After all tests, finish deferred jobs shortest first.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}

Both algorithms follow the same principle: process short jobs as soon as
possible and leave long jobs for the end.  The difficult part is choosing the
threshold.  A deterministic algorithm has no representative view of the
unseen jobs, so its threshold has to change with the history.  The exact
update is given in steps 3 and 4 of
\cref{alg:intro-obligatory-det}.  The lower bound explains this moving
threshold.  It reveals positive jobs in decreasing levels while hiding many
zero jobs until the end.  Processing a positive level immediately delays the
hidden zeros; postponing it instead creates a costly tail.  The levels can be
chosen so that these two losses balance at $1.57\ldots$; the formal
construction is the cap-free core in \cref{sec:lower}, and
\cref{sec:obligatory} transfers the resulting plateau to obligatory testing.

Randomization gives the algorithm a global view of the input.  It first tests
a sublinear random sample and estimates the distribution of job lengths.
This costs only $o(n^2)$ and therefore does not change the asymptotic ratio.
Up to a small estimation error, the algorithm now knows the remaining input
distribution and can keep one fixed threshold for the rest of the schedule.  The
earlier obligatory-testing work studied deterministic algorithms and
explicitly left randomization open~\cite{DogeasErlebachLiang2024}.

The matching randomized lower bound is correspondingly simple.  Half of the
jobs have length zero and half have length two, and their labels are shuffled.
Until the shuffle is uncovered, one unit of work completes only about half a
job.  Counting the area under the number of unfinished jobs gives the lower
bound $4/3$.  The formal argument is in \cref{sec:random-obligatory}.

\subsection{One algorithm for every input}
\label{sec:intro-obligatory-instance}

Let us return to \cref{alg:intro-obligatory-rand}.  The ratio $4/3$ describes
only the hardest input.  It says much less on an easy one.  Suppose, for
example, that all processing times are very large compared with the unit
test.  Then the simple strategy that first tests everything and subsequently
runs the jobs shortest first is nearly optimal.  A worst-case guarantee still
tells us only that our cost is at most $1.33\ldots$ times the optimum.

A slight variant\footnote{ The variant maximizes
over thresholds up to $O(n^{1/20})$, rather than up to a fixed constant.  It
is still worst-case optimal, with competitive ratio $4/3$; we presented the
fixed-cutoff algorithm first because its worst-case analysis is simpler.} of \cref{alg:intro-obligatory-rand} satisfies a much stronger
per-input guarantee: it is \emph{instance-optimal}.

\begin{theorem}[Obligatory-testing instance optimality, informal version of
\protect\cref{thm:obligatory-instance}]
\label{thm:intro-obligatory-instance}
Fix $L<\infty$.  There is a variant $\mathcal A^*$ of
\cref{alg:intro-obligatory-rand} such that, for every size-$n$ multiset
$M\subseteq[0,L]$ and every online algorithm $\mathcal A'$,
\[
 \cost(\mathcal A^*)\le \cost(\mathcal A')+o_L(n^2),
\]
where both costs are measured on $M$ and include the algorithms' randomness.
The competing algorithm $\mathcal A'$ may even be designed specifically for
$M$ and told the entire multiset in advance, though not its assignment to
the public labels.
\end{theorem}

Here and in the instance-optimal statements below, cost on a multiset uses
the order-oblivious convention of \cref{rem:order-oblivious}.  A private
shuffle makes the original labeling irrelevant, so the comparison is made
separately for every multiset $M$.

Both assumptions in the theorem are necessary.  Without a fixed bound $L$
there is no randomized instance-optimal algorithm, and without randomization
there is no deterministic one; see \cref{thm:ot-instance-impossibility}.

Two points about the theorem are worth emphasizing.  First, the comparison is made separately
on every multiset.  We must therefore compete not only with one fixed online
algorithm, but also with algorithms optimized for this particular $M$.
Allowing $\mathcal A'$ to know $M$ makes this strongest possible online
benchmark explicit.

Hence, the existence of some instance-optimal algorithm is perhaps not so
surprising.  One could imagine a meta-algorithm that samples the input to
estimate $M$ and then runs an optimal policy for that estimate.  The
substantive part of the theorem is that no complicated meta-algorithm is
needed.  The optimal leading-order policy for a known input is itself a simple
threshold policy, so $\mathcal A^*$ has essentially the same form as
\cref{alg:intro-obligatory-rand}: sample the input distribution, choose the
maximum-density threshold, and use it for the rest of the schedule.


\subsection{Generalizations of the model}
\label{sec:intro-optional-map}

We next weaken the obligatory model in two directions.  First, we may allow a
job to run without being tested.  Second, the unit preliminary action may have
two different benefits.  It may reveal the true running time $p_i$ before the
job is run.  It may also be an optimization procedure that replaces a common
raw running time $u$ by a shorter time $p_i\leq u$.  For example, a generic
piece of code solves every task within time $u$, but an AI agent can spend one
unit adapting the code to a particular input and make it faster.

These two benefits are independent.  An optimization may reveal its new
running time $p_i$, giving the scheduler both a faster job and information
about where the job belongs.  Or it may leave $p_i$ hidden until the optimized
job finishes.

These choices give the following four models.  The table is a summary rather
than four new sets of notation; in every row, the preliminary action in the
third column costs one unit of machine time.

The last three rows separate the two benefits of the unit preliminary action.
Blind execution studies information without speedup.  Blind optimization
studies speedup without advance information.  Revealing optimization contains
both benefits.  This model was introduced by D{\"u}rr et
al.~\cite{DurrErlebachMegowMeissner2020}.  Blind execution is an
adversarial-input counterpart of stochastic scheduling with testing
~\cite{LeviMagnantiShaposhnik2019}; its closest earlier adversarial relative
is the binary processing-time oracle of Dufoss{\'e} et
al.~\cite{DufosseEtAl2022}.  Blind optimization is
closest to the non-revealing optimization studied by Damerius et
al.~\cite{DameriusEtAl2023}, although their optimization consumes a separate
budget rather than machine time.

The three optional models have different optimal policies and different
input-specific benchmarks.  We therefore state their results separately.  The
algorithms nevertheless share one simple design principle: privately shuffle
the jobs, use a sublinear sample to estimate the input distribution, and optimize the rest of
the schedule as if that estimate were exact.

\subsection{Information without speedup: blind execution}
\label{sec:intro-blind}

Suppose an untested job can simply be run until it finishes.  Its running
time is its true hidden length $p_i$.  Testing does not make the job shorter;
it only tells us $p_i$ before we decide when to run it.  We call this
\emph{blind execution}.

Without an upper bound on the job lengths, no randomized algorithm has a
finite competitive ratio, even if we ignore an additive $o(n^2)$ term; see
\cref{thm:blind-unbounded-impossible}.  For every fixed bound $L$, however,
sampling again gives one algorithm that is optimal on every bounded multiset.


\begin{theorem}[Blind-execution instance optimality, informal version of
\protect\cref{thm:blind-instance}]
\label{thm:intro-blind-instance}
Fix $L<\infty$.  There is one randomized algorithm $\mathcal A^*$ such that,
for every size-$n$ multiset $M\subseteq[0,L]$ and every online algorithm $\mathcal A'$,
\[
 \cost(\mathcal A^*)\le \cost(\mathcal A')+o_L(n^2).
\]
\end{theorem}

To describe the algorithm, let $D$ be the empirical distribution of job
lengths, let $P\sim D$, and put $\mu=\mathbb E P$.  Testing is initially
worthwhile when the threshold $\tau_D$ determined by
\[
 \mathbb E(\tau_D-P)^+=1
\]
is smaller than $\mu$.  Even then, it need not be optimal to test the entire
input.  The leading cost of testing a fraction $q$ is an explicit quadratic
$F_{\mathsf{BE},D}(q)$, given in \cref{eq:blind-F}, and the algorithm chooses its minimum
over $0\le q\le1$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Instance-optimal blind execution}
\label{alg:intro-blind}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Shuffle the jobs and run $o(n)$ of them blindly; use their observed
lengths to estimate $D$.
\State Find $\tau_D$ from $\mathbb E(\tau_D-P)^+=1$.
\State If $\tau_D\ge\mu$, put $q=0$.  Otherwise
$q\in\arg\min_{0\le z\le1}F_{\mathsf{BE},D}(z)$.
\State Test a random $q$-fraction of the remaining jobs.  Whenever a test
reveals $p<\tau_D$, process that job immediately; defer all other outcomes.
\State After the tests stop, process the deferred jobs with
$\tau_D\le p<\mu$, shortest first.
\State Run every untouched job blindly.
\State Finish the remaining tested jobs shortest first.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The block between testing and blind execution is important.  A job can be too
long to interrupt the test phase and still be shorter than an untouched job
on average.  Such a job waits until testing stops and is then processed before
the blind block.


\subsection{Blind optimization}
\label{sec:intro-blind-optimization}

The two remaining models start from the same operation.  Every job takes a
known raw time $u$, and one unit of optimization reduces its running time to
$p_i\leq u$.  They differ only in whether optimization reveals $p_i$.  We
start with the case in which it does not.  Before execution, all optimized
jobs still look the same.

This is the simplest case.  Randomization is necessary for instance
optimality: a binary adversarial-labeling argument rules out every
deterministic policy; see \cref{thm:bo-det-not-instance-optimal}.
The deterministic worst-case problem nevertheless has an exact and very
simple answer.  Up to $u=2$, run every job raw.  After $u=2$, optimize and
run every job immediately in an arbitrary fixed order.  A hidden-stopping
adversary proves that adaptively mixing the two actions cannot improve this
choice.

\begin{theorem}[Deterministic blind-optimization curve, informal version of
\protect\cref{thm:bo-det-curve}]
\label{thm:intro-bo-det-curve}
The exact deterministic asymptotic competitive ratio is
\begin{equation}
 \RdetBO(u)=
 \begin{cases}
  1,&0<u\le1,\\[2pt]
  u,&1\le u\le2,\\[5pt]
  \displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{4u^3-4u+1}-1}{2(u-1)},&u\ge2.
 \end{cases}
 \label{eq:intro-bo-det-curve}
\end{equation}
It grows as $\sqrt u+o(1)$.
\end{theorem}

With randomization, we can sample a few jobs to estimate the only number that
matters: the mean $\mu$ of the optimized lengths.  The policy in
\cref{alg:intro-blind-optimization} then compares
the two average times per job, $u$ and $1+\mu$, and uses the better action for
all remaining jobs.  

\begin{theorem}[Blind-optimization instance optimality, informal version of
\protect\cref{thm:bo-instance}]
\label{thm:intro-bo-instance}
Consider the blind optimization model with some $u<\infty$.  There is one randomized algorithm $\mathcal A^*$ such that,
for every size-$n$ multiset $M\subseteq[0,u]$ and every online algorithm $\mathcal A'$,
\[
 \cost(\mathcal A^*)\le \cost(\mathcal A')+o_u(n^2).
\]
\end{theorem}


\noindent
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.485\textwidth}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Instance-optimal blind optimization}
\label{alg:intro-blind-optimization}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Shuffle the jobs, optimize and run $\lceil n^{2/3}\rceil$ of them,
and let $\widehat\mu$ be the mean observed length.
\State Treat $\widehat\mu$ as the true mean of the input.
\State If $1+\widehat\mu<u$, optimize and then run every remaining job.
\State Otherwise run every remaining job raw for time $u$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}[t]{0.485\textwidth}
\vspace{0pt}
\centering
\input{blind_optimization_curve_tikz}
\captionof{figure}{The exact deterministic and randomized ratios for blind
optimization.}
\label{fig:intro-bo-curve}
\end{minipage}

More precisely, if $\mu_M$ is the mean optimized length in $M$, the best
possible leading value is
\[
 \frac{n^2}{2}\min\{u,1+\mu_M\}.
\]
Knowing the instance-optimal algorithm does not yet give its competitive
ratio: we must still find the worst input.  The resulting ratio is unbounded
and grows on the order of $\sqrt u$.

\begin{theorem}[Blind-optimization curve, informal version of
\protect\cref{thm:bo-curve}]
\label{thm:intro-bo-curve}
The exact randomized asymptotic competitive ratio $\RrandBO(u)$ is $1$ for $u\le1$ and is
given by the explicit two-branch curve in
\cref{fig:intro-bo-curve} for $u>1$.  It grows as
$\sqrt u/2+1/2+o(1)$ as $u\to\infty$.
\end{theorem}



\subsection{Revealing optimization}
\label{sec:intro-revealing-optimization}

Now assume that optimization also reveals $p_i$.  Thus the unit action buys
both information and speed.  We call this \emph{revealing optimization}.

The two extreme values of $u$ are easy to understand.  If $u\leq1$, the test alone costs
at least as much as raw execution, so we should never test.  If $u$ gets very
large, raw execution is unattractive, so the problem approaches obligatory
testing.  The interesting question is what happens between these endpoints.

This model was introduced by D{\"u}rr et
al.~\cite{DurrErlebachMegowMeissner2020}.  Their results already showed that
intermediate values of $u$ are the difficult ones.  We determine the exact
answer for every $u$, for both deterministic and randomized algorithms.


\begin{theorem}[Revealing-optimization curves, informal version of
\protect\cref{thm:optional-curve,thm:random-common-upper}]
\label{thm:intro-common-upper-curves}
For every $u>0$, the exact deterministic and randomized asymptotic
competitive ratios $\RdetRO(u)$ and $\RrandRO(u)$ are the six-piece and four-piece curves in
\cref{fig:intro-curves-det,fig:intro-curves-rand}.  The deterministic curve reaches
$1.87\ldots$ and then falls to the obligatory value
$1.57\ldots$.  The randomized curve reaches $1.63\ldots$ and
eventually becomes $4/3$.
\end{theorem} 

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\input{curves_overview_tikz}
\caption{The three proved deterministic policy guarantees and their exact
lower envelope.  The black outline is the optimal ratio.  The arrow on
\textsc{Raw} marks its off-scale linear continuation; the teal horizontal
line is the $\varphi$ guarantee of zero-prefix \textsc{ForcedPrefixUTE}.}
\label{fig:intro-curves-det}
\end{figure}

The deterministic curve is the lower envelope of three explicit policy
families.  We describe their shape here and defer the exact parameters to
\cref{sec:optional}.

The first algorithm, \textsc{Raw}$(u)$, simply fixes an arbitrary job order
and executes every job untested for the public raw time $u$.  Its ratio is
$1$ for $u\le1$ and $u$ for $u>1$, and it supplies the lower envelope up to
$\udiam=1.866760\ldots$; see \cref{fig:intro-curves-det}.

The second algorithm, \textsc{ForcedPrefixUTE} in
\cref{alg:intro-ro-ute}, tests every job and normally processes only
outcomes below a fixed threshold.  It makes one deliberate exception: every
outcome in an initial $b(u)$-fraction of the test order is processed
immediately.  Without this forced prefix, deferring early long outcomes can
create a tail that is delayed by almost all later tests; the prefix balances
this tail cost against the opposite risk of processing a long job before
shorter unseen jobs.

The exact parameter $b(u)$ is defined in
\cref{eq:opt:ute-parameters}; it is nonnegative on
$[\udiam,\uzero]$ and vanishes at $\uzero$.
For $u\ge\varphi+2$, the same zero-prefix policy remains
$\varphi$-competitive by \cref{lem:opt:zero-prefix}; this continuation is
shown in \cref{fig:intro-curves-det} to make the lower envelope visible.

\begin{algorithm}[htbp]
\caption{\textsc{ForcedPrefixUTE}$(J,u)$}
\label{alg:intro-ro-ute}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Fix a test order $j_1,\ldots,j_n$ and set $Q\gets\varnothing$.
\State Set $\theta_u\gets\min\{1,u-1\}$.  If
$\udiam\le u\le\uzero$, set $b\gets b(u)$; otherwise set $b\gets0$.
\State For $i=1,\ldots,n$, test $j_i$ and observe $p_{j_i}$.
\State \quad If $i\le\lfloor bn\rfloor$ or $p_{j_i}\le\theta_u$, process
$j_i$ immediately; otherwise add it to $Q$.
\State Process the jobs of $Q$ in nondecreasing revealed processing time.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Finally, \textsc{AdaptiveThreshold} is the same history-dependent policy as
\cref{alg:intro-obligatory-det}.  For finite $u$, only its parameter $c$
changes: on $\varphi+2\le u\le \zstar=4.505241\ldots$ it is chosen by the
optimization in \cref{eq:opt:mixed-parametrization}, after which it equals
the obligatory-testing value $0.570574\ldots$.  The three policies form the
deterministic lower envelope in \cref{fig:intro-curves-det}:
\textsc{Raw} wins first, \textsc{ForcedPrefixUTE} in the middle, and
\textsc{AdaptiveThreshold} for large $u$.


For randomized algorithms, private sampling again gives instance optimality
with a policy similar in shape to \cref{alg:intro-blind}.  Unlike blind
execution, every tested processing time is at most the raw time $u$, so all
tested jobs belong before the untouched raw block.


\begin{theorem}[Revealing-optimization instance optimality, informal version of
\protect\cref{thm:common-upper-instance}]
\label{thm:intro-common-upper-instance}
Fix $u<\infty$.  There is one randomized algorithm $\mathcal A^*$ such that,
for every size-$n$ multiset $M\subseteq[0,u]$ and every online algorithm $\mathcal A'$,
\[
 \cost(\mathcal A^*)\le \cost(\mathcal A')+o_u(n^2).
\]

\end{theorem}

\vspace{0pt}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Instance-optimal revealing-optimization policy}
\label{alg:intro-common-upper}
\begin{algorithmic}
\State Shuffle the jobs, test $\lceil\sqrt n\rceil$ of them, and use the
observed lengths to estimate the input distribution.
\State Compute the best threshold and the best tested fraction $q$.
\State Test a random $q$-fraction; finish outcomes below the threshold at once.
\State Finish all other tested jobs, shortest first.
\State Run every untouched job raw for time $u$.
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{minipage}\hfill

\vspace{0pt}

\captionof{figure}{The exact randomized revealing-optimization ratio as a function of

The pilot sample estimates the input distribution.  If that estimate were
exact, the best leading-order online schedule would be determined by two
numbers: a threshold and the fraction $q$ of jobs to test.  Short outcomes
are completed during testing, longer tested outcomes form a shortest-first
tail, and the untouched jobs form the final raw block.  The algorithm
optimizes these two parameters on the sample and applies them after a private
shuffle.  The sample and its estimation error cost only $o_u(n^2)$.

For each multiset, this policy attains the best online leading cost.  The
competitive ratio is obtained by maximizing the ratio between that cost and
the clairvoyant optimum over inputs.  The worst input can be reduced to two
job lengths, leaving a one-variable optimization.

The curves in \cref{fig:intro-curves-det,fig:intro-curves-rand} are not
monotone.  This is not a contradiction.  For very small $u$, testing is
useless, while as $u\to\infty$ the model converges to the obligatory-testing
setting of \cref{sec:obligatory}.  Intermediate values are harder because
raw execution gives the clairvoyant scheduler an additional useful option.

The exact formulas and matching constructions are in
\cref{sec:optional,sec:random-common-upper}.

\subsection{Techniques}

The proofs repeatedly use three pictures.

\paragraph{The area of unfinished jobs.}
During one unit of machine work, the objective increases by the number of
jobs that are still unfinished.  Thus the sum of completion times is the area
under the curve ``number of unfinished jobs versus time.''  This picture is
often easier to reason about than individual completion times.

\paragraph{Jobs completed per unit of work.}
A round of tests together with the short jobs completed during that round can
be viewed as one large action.  It spends some amount of time and completes
some fraction of the jobs.  Higher-density actions---those completing more
jobs per unit of work---should come first.  Sorting these actions produces
the thresholds, the ordered blocks, and the one-variable quadratics in the
instance-optimal results.

\paragraph{A private shuffle.}
The universal algorithms start by shuffling the labels and inspecting a
small prefix.  The same idea is needed in the lower bound, where the policy
may adaptively decide which label to touch next.  If equal job lengths are
temporarily distinguished as separate tokens, the order in which any
deterministic policy first reveals these tokens is itself just a permutation
of them.  The revealed sequence is therefore sampling without replacement.
One concentration event can then control every possible stopping point of
the policy.

The deterministic proofs use a complementary idea.  Their lower bounds make
the algorithm choose between completing the current jobs and waiting for
shorter future jobs.  Their upper bounds maintain a threshold and charge each
wrongly ordered pair to a small potential.  The exact constants appear when
the two possible losses are equal.

\subsection{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The closest papers have already appeared next to the models they introduced.
We give a broader comparison here.

Scheduling with tests belongs to optimization with \emph{explorable
uncertainty}, where uncertain data can be queried at a cost; the query
viewpoint goes back at least to Kahan~\cite{Kahan1991}.  Levi, Magnanti, and
Shaposhnik study a stochastic scheduling model and derive prefix-testing
policies followed by an order based on expected processing time per unit
weight~\cite{LeviMagnantiShaposhnik2019,LeviMagnantiShaposhnik2024}.  Our
four-block blind-execution policy has a similar shape, but our inputs are
adversarial, need not converge to a distribution, and are not announced to
the universal algorithm.

D{\"u}rr et al.~\cite{DurrErlebachMegowMeissner2020} introduced the
adversarial revealing-optimization model and obtained deterministic bounds, including
the $1.87\ldots$ guarantee for binary values.  We close the deterministic
gap for arbitrary values $p_i\in[0,u]$ and give the exact randomized curve
against an oblivious adversary.  Related work allows nonuniform test
times~\cite{AlbersEckl2021,LiuLiuWongZhang2023,KrekelbergEtAl2026}, multiple
machines~\cite{GongChenHayashi2024}, or weights~\cite{BuldSchulz2026}.

Dogeas, Erlebach, and Liang~\cite{DogeasErlebachLiang2024} introduced
obligatory testing for total completion time.  For uniform tests they proved
the SIDLE upper bound $1.58\ldots$ and the deterministic lower bound
$\sqrt2$.  Liang and Liang~\cite{LiangLiang2026} later raised the lower bound
to $3/2$.  Our matching value is $1.57\ldots$, and our randomized
result gives the oblivious value $4/3$.  More strongly, its stationary
threshold is asymptotically optimal separately for every bounded input.

The processing-time oracle of Dufoss{\'e} et al.~\cite{DufosseEtAl2022} is
close to blind execution, but allows only two possible hidden durations.  If
no tests are available and processing times are revealed only at completion,
one obtains the classical nonclairvoyant model of Motwani, Phillips, and
Torng~\cite{MotwaniPhillipsTorng1994}.  These semantics differ from raw
execution in revealing optimization, which always lasts the declared time
$u$.

\subsection{Roadmap}
\label{sec:roadmap}

\Cref{sec:model} formally defines the four models, their information
structures and adversaries, the corresponding offline optima, and our
asymptotic competitive-ratio convention.

\Cref{sec:optional} then proves the exact deterministic
revealing-optimization curve.  Its cap-free harmonic core and parameterized
endpoint bank are shared by the mixed branch and the plateau; only after the
finite curve is complete does \cref{sec:obligatory} derive the deterministic
obligatory ratio by a uniform endpoint transfer.

\Cref{sec:common-upper-instance} identifies the optimal leading cost
separately for every bounded revealing input and learns the associated
threshold policy from a sublinear sample.  \Cref{sec:random-common-upper}
maximizes this benchmark against the clairvoyant optimum and proves the exact
four-piece randomized curve.  Its final subsection,
\cref{sec:random-obligatory}, transfers the lower bound to the unbounded
obligatory endpoint and supplies the cutoff-and-fallback argument needed for
a uniform $4/3$ upper bound.  \Cref{sec:obligatory-instance} then strengthens
that endpoint algorithm to a single policy that is asymptotically optimal on
every bounded obligatory input.

\Cref{sec:blind} turns to blind execution.  It derives the announced-input
benchmark, proves that arbitrary adaptive policies cannot beat it, and
removes advance knowledge of the input by sampling; it also explains why no
finite worst-case ratio is possible without a bound on job lengths.
\Cref{sec:blind-optimization} studies speedup without revelation, proves the
deterministic hidden-stopping lower bound and matching two-policy curve,
proves the randomized instance-optimal theorem, and maximizes its mean-based
benchmark to obtain the exact randomized worst-case curve.

The appendices document and independently audit the more delicate parts of
the argument.  \Cref{sec:formalization} states precisely which results are
machine checked and summarizes the corresponding Lean~4 development.
\Cref{app:finite-checkpoint} evaluates a large but finite rational instance
of the cap-free lower bound, while \cref{app:cap-bubbling} gives an
independent cap-bubbling certificate for the deterministic upper bound.
Finally, \cref{app:lean-formalization} records the exported Lean theorem and
the detailed dictionary between its objects and the deterministic statements
in the paper.
